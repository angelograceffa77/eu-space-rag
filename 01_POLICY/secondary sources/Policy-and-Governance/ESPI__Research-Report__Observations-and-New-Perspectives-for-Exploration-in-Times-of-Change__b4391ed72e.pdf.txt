FOREWORD
In March 2023, “Revolution 
Space”, a report elaborated 
by an independent High-Level 
Advisory Group was presented 
to ESA Council by Anders Fogh 
Rasmussen former Danish Prime 
Minister and Secretary General 
of NATO. It called for Europe to 
become a leader in space exploration, 
emphasizing the need for Europe to 
strengthen its autonomy, resilience, and 
competitiveness in space. The report 
warned that otherwise Europe may “fall 
behind and become dependent of others”. 
Like many wake-up calls, that call for a revolution, 
to ACT NOW remained unanswered. Two years later, 
reality has overtaken what appeared visionary then. 
Seismic geo-political and geo-economic shifts put the 
future of decades of trans-Atlantic partnership and the future 
of NASA into question. The weakness of Europe’s traditional 
role as junior partner is fully exposed. 
As a first reaction, efforts aiming at the diversification of partnerships 
takes new prominence, as Europe also turns East. The challenge may 
turn into a unique opportunity for Europe, and it now is imperative that 
European policy makers and agencies, together with industry must 
act to develop the full value of space exploration for innovation and 
competitiveness of Europe.

IMPACT CASE 1: 
In-space additive manufacturing
Given the different technological requirements for operating in microgravity, human presence in LEO 
requires different approaches to problem-solving which are true for the broader space sector. Specific 
capabilities, such as in-space additive manufacturing, which originated as a support capability to 
assist astronauts in daily tasks, now have technological implications that could help reduce launch 
costs and revolutionise in-orbit assembly and manufacturing. For instance, breakthrough technologies 
such as the Additive Manufacturing Facility built by Made In Space (now Redwire), which were tested 
and operated aboard the ISS marked an important milestone for ISAM and exploration as a whole.

IMPACT CASE 2:
In-orbit refuelling
Exploration-enabled technologies, like 
in-orbit refuelling, hold transformative 
potential for the space economy. By 
leveraging platforms like the ISS, 
commercial actors, especially in the 
US, not only validate their technologies 
but also position themselves to capture 
emerging market segments. The 
growing collaboration between U.S. and 
European stakeholders, exemplified by 
Orbit Fab’s integration into European operations, underscores exploration’s far-reaching economic 
impacts across the global space sector, which is not always fully captured by European companies.
IMPACT CASE 3: 
International Competitiveness
International cooperation in exploration, historically with NASA, was the 
determining factor of ESA’s exploration heritage. The construction of exploration 
modules such as SPACELAB and MPLM was key in defining and nurturing European 
industry’s technical expertise and played an important role in maintaining a 
European foothold in the US market until now. This critical juncture is what so 
far kept European industrial players attractive contractors for US exploration and 
non-exploration initiatives. Both Airbus and Thales have drawn significant profits 
from exploration programmes, representing 15% of their annual revenue in 2022. 2 
Although their status as European champions and their large involvement in ESA 
programmes helps them maintain their status quo, it’s this technical expertise that 
has thus far allowed them to win US commercial contracts outside of ESA-NASA 
collaborations.

SPACE EXPLORATION DRIVES THE INSPIRATIONAL POWER OF SPACE TALENTS4 EXPLORATION DRIVES A LOYAL, COMMITTED, AND PURPOSEFUL WORKFORCE
Space Exploration (together with Space Science and Technology) 
are the main factors contributing to the inspirational power of the 
space sector, while the more concrete space applications on earth 
(economy, security, climate, diplomacy) fare significantly lower in 
comparison. About 55% of respondents that are inspired by space 
in general, are inspired or interested by the former, while only 
about 30% have a fascination for the latter.
The captivating pull of space exploration in retaining individuals within the space sector is 
reflected indirectly in their salary expectations and willingness to also work in other industries.  
Inspiration plays a stronger role in attracting people to the space sector than the direct monetary 
compensation. This relationship is strongly influenced by the inspirational power of space exploration 
specifically. Students and young professionals inspired by Exploration have significantly lower 
salary expectation for junior positions than the ones driven by other parts of the space domain. This 
lower salary expectations hints that space exploration creates a stronger internal motivation 
and purpose for young talent to work in the space sector compared to other domains. 
Additionally, people who are inspired through exploration seem to generally be more loyal and 
committed to the space sector. The share of people inspired through either HSF or M&M, who can 
imagine also working in other sectors, is significantly lower than for other, more earth-related 
inspirators, such as economy, sustainability, or security. These individuals are less likely to apply to 
jobs outside the space sector.
