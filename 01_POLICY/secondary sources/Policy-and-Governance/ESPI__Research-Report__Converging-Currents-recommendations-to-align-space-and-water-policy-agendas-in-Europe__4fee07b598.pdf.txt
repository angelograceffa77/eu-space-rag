his study identifies practical pathways for a stronger alignment between space and water policy priorities. It 
aims at informing ongoing and future policy processes, including the initiatives conducted in the framework of 
the European Water Resilience Strategy and the UN Water Action Agenda. 
The results are derived from an analysis of national freshwater policy priorities across 32 European countries, as 
well as a compilation and review of over 250 primarily European-led downstream projects leveraging space data 
and infrastructure for freshwater-related applications, complemented by insights from stakeholder consultations. 
CONVERGING CURRENTS executive summary
At the intersection of the space and water policy 
communities, dialogue occurs across three main 
pathways: 1) top-down, initiated by national ministries 
or transboundary river basin authorities seeking 
technical support from international organisations, 
which connect requests with relevant geospatial 
expertise and networks (including international water 
conventions); 2) bottom-up , through satellite-based 
service coordinators engaging local water and disaster 
management actors, building capacity for using space-
derived data and creating opportunities for service co-
design, for instance hydrological predictions; 3) on the 
ground, with local actors supporting space applications 
by providing access to field sites, as well as in-situ 
information for satellite calibration and data validation.
Overall, a contrasted policy-project alignment is observed, 
with the level of prioritisation of water policy objectives 
being unevenly addressed by satellite solutions, in number 
and size.
Key opportunities to expand space solutions emerge. In 
particular, the value of space data seems to be overlooked 
for ground-level operations in water supply and urban 
wastewater management, despite demonstrated potential 
(e.g., leak detection, water reservoir stability monitoring, 
detection of untreated sewage contaminating water 
resources). The focus of space solutions on floods, droughts 
and irrigation management instead suggests that the space 
sector remains largely focused on established use cases 
and “passive” (yet essential) monitoring, and has yet to 
transition towards more “proactive” solutions addressing 
less-explored operational needs.
Water diplomacy has emerged as a high-level policy 
concept reflected in a significant number of water policies, 
some specifically dedicated to it. However, it has yet 
to be explicitly recognised as an objective that space-
based solutions can actively support, both within space 
policies and as the target of satellite solutions. In many 
transboundary contexts where negotiations over shared 
water resources management are hindered by either the 
absence of data, outdated data, or disagreements over 
the credibility of datasets, stakeholders consistently 
emphasised the current and potential value of Earth 
observation as a trusted, third-party information source, 
helping to reduce information asymmetries and build trust 
between riparian states.

Awareness of space applications within the water sector is high, but water-space policy alignment remains 
limited. While nearly 60% of analysed national water policies reference space technologies, mentions of water 
in space policies remain, in comparison, limited. Awareness gaps remain, as 20% of water policies mention only 
non-space technologies such as AI or drones. Overall, there is limited correlation between policy cross-reference 
and space projects implementation, which suggests that water and space policies evolve largely in parallel 
with limited coordination: water policies emphasise technological applications for water resource management 
(e.g., urban wastewater), while space policies tend to prioritise established use cases where the value of space 
is already demonstrated (e.g., droughts and inland flooding), rather than less-explored application areas (e.g., 
drinking water production, water supply management). 
River basin organisations remain underrepresented in the consortium of space projects despite their strategic 
role in water management.  Uniquely positioned to support national and transboundary water management, 
they bring valuable governance experience, GIS/HIS datasets, and long-term institutional knowledge. 
Integration of satellite data with these organisations’ ground observations would strengthen the work of 
river basin organisations beyond flood and drought forecasting, for freshwater ecosystem management and 
water quality; this requires capacity building, peer learning, and resource support to address differing basin 
challenges.
Recent water policies (2019-2025) increasingly emphasise water security, encompassing water availability, 
water infrastructure resilience and water diplomacy, yet these priorities remain insufficiently addressed by 
space projects. Despite its policy prominence, water infrastructure resilience features in a few projects, leaving 
opportunities in water supply management, water-sensitive urban design and drinking water production. 
Stakeholders noted that space data’s value is often overlooked in these activities traditionally viewed as 
ground-level operations, despite proven applications (e.g., reservoir monitoring, predictive maintenance 
of pipe networks). Similarly, while nearly 60% of space projects have an international impact reflecting the 
transboundary nature of water resources, water diplomacy remains largely absent from space policies 
and projects, despite being prioritised by nearly 30% of countries and the demonstrated value of space for 
transboundary cooperation.
Private companies offer untapped opportunities to finance and scale up water-related space solutions.  
Private water actors (e.g., private water suppliers, water management consultancies), involved in only 20% of 
projects analysed with little overlap with public water actors, provide access to end-users who can help design 
and finance solutions (e.g., insurers, large producers). Water-dependent companies also have incentives to 
reduce their environmental footprint and support river basin preservation and restoration, as their degradation 
can be a consequence of their activities and a direct threat to their continuity. Private companies sometimes 
also sponsor publicly developed, open-access space-based platforms, stimulating downstream demand. 
Space data delivers the greatest value when combined with in-situ data and embedded in broader 
governance frameworks. Stakeholders cautioned that adopting an exclusively technology-driven approach to 
freshwater management without recognising where ground data becomes necessary would  risk “overselling” 
the potential of contribution of space technologies to freshwater applications, potentially leading to misdirected 
investments. Additionally, they underscored how such a technology-driven approach may trigger rebound 
effects: in agriculture, for instance, tools enabling more efficient water use often lead to the expansion of the 
cultivated area instead of reducing overall water consumption, especially when policy measures to reduce 
consumption remain limited. 
FINDINGS
Water sector actors, public and private, are involved in only slightly over 25% of the consortia of analysed 
satellite-based projects. Public water actors (e.g., municipalities, public agencies), despite representing nearly 
half of primary end-users, are mostly absent from project consortia, creating a risk of technology providers 
disconnecting from implementation needs. Private water actors (e.g., water supply companies) remain also 
largely absent: when involved, they help connecting solutions with a broader spectrum of end-users, including 
hydropower producers, water supply companies and small-holder farmers.
Water management governance is decentralised, with implementation often occurring at basin and municipal 
levels. However, nearly 60% of analysed space projects are deployed at continent or global scale. While global, 
open-access platforms provide shared tools and datasets, local actors need tailored applications reflecting 
specific governance arrangements (e.g., river basin authorities) and water management challenges (e.g., wetland 
health monitoring). Limited funding for stakeholder mapping during project design reduces understanding of 
institutional landscape and implementation needs.

Space solutions should be designed around the decentralised nature of water governance frameworks, 
informed by funded and mandatory stakeholder mapping. To enable this process, local and regional 
water actors should act as intermediaries to translate end-user needs into tailored solutions.

River basin organisations should be positioned as co-designers of space projects for water management, 
leveraging existing networks and peer-learning initiatives. Sustained dialogue with space providers

Space-based services should complement, rather than substitute, in-situ data and local expertise. 
Integrating space and ground-based data will support adoption, while pairing investments with policy 
reforms on water allocation, pricing and abstraction limits will ensure that efficiency gains genuinely 
improve freshwater management.

RECOMMENDATIONS
Public and private water actors should be systematically involved as co-designers of water-related 
space projects rather than only consultees. Involving end-users throughout project development would 
strengthen trust, operational relevance and buy-in of solutions. 
CO-DESIGNING SPACE SOLUTIONS WITH WATER SECTOR ACTORS 
2
To encourage the development of solutions that directly address existing water policy challenges 
and improve the translation of policy priorities into space projects, there is a need to align cross-
references between space and water policies around shared priority areas, where relevant. National 
space policies should explicitly recognise water as a strategic application area, while in countries 
promoting technology-enabled water solutions without reference to space, strengthening space 
considerations in water policies can help create entry points for the development of satellite-based 
solutions aligned by design with sectoral priorities. 
POSITIONING SPACE AS A CORE COMPONENT OF WATER MANAGEMENT SOLUTIONS

The space sector should increasingly develop solutions addressing the operational needs of the water 
sector, particularly the security and resilience of water infrastructure. This should include stronger 
engagement at municipal level, where water supply is primarily managed, in anticipation of the 
growing prominence of water security in future policies. In parallel, the role of space technologies in 
diplomatic efforts and cooperation over shared water resources should be more explicitly recognised 
and translated into concrete objectives, both in water and space policies, and at implementation level.

1 WATER HAS NO ENEMY: CONNECTING DATA AND RESILIENCE 
Access to water for domestic and personal use is a fundamental human right,1 yet water 
resources face growing pressure from scarcity and increasing extreme events affecting 
many sectors, including impacts on  energy production.2 In 2026, the concept of  “Global 
Water Bankruptcy”, further highlighted the depletion of both  renewable water "income" 
from rivers and soils, and long-term "savings" held in aquifers, glaciers, and wetlands. 3 
Meanwhile, previous ESPI research identiﬁed freshwater use cases such as water quality 
as high priorities within climate resilience and agriculture policies.4   
As the Nigerian songwriter Fela Kuti sang, water has no enemy,5 especially when observed 
from the bird’s-eye-view perspective of satellites.  Water is an essential shared resource 
that transcends individual nations or sectors. Investing in water means investing in health, 
biodiversity, the energy transition, climate resilience, food security, peace and diplomacy. 
More than ever, effectively addressing water challenges depends on the availability and 
integration of timely  and spatially comprehensive data. Space technologies – Earth 
observation (EO),6 Positioning, Navigation & Timing (PNT ) and Satellite communications 
(Satcom) – already support European policy priorities across domains, including 
freshwater management.7 They enable the monitoring, forecasting, and transboundary 
cooperation required for sustainable water governance.  Recent missions like ESA’s 
HydroGNSS measure key hydrological climate variables, including soil moisture, wetland 
inundation and freeze-thaw states. Upcoming space missions like LSTM, CHIME and ROSE-
L will further enhance the monitoring of evapotranspiration, land and surface water 
temperature, lake ecosystems, and the cryosphere.   
This study presents a deep dive into space solutions for the freshwater domain,  
building on the ﬁndings of ESPI's report Where Sensing Makes Sense — Opportunities 
for Advancing Europe's Priorities through Earth Observation, 8 developed in 
collaboration with the European Space Agency (ESA). The report identiﬁed water as 
one of the priority policy domains with strong potential for collaboration with the 
space sector and highlighted the role of space as an enabler of water policy objectives.

1.1 Objectives and content of the study  
The study identiﬁes practical pathways for space and water policymakers to better align 
their agendas and accelerate the development and uptake of space -enabled solutions 
for water policy objectives. While ESPI’s previous research analysed how Earth Observation 
could address European policy priorities across domains (e.g., energy, agriculture, climate 
resilience), the present study focuses on a single sector : water, whose speciﬁc standards 
and operational logic call for a dedicated assessment. To this end, the study provides: 
• An analysis of national freshwater policy priorities; 
• A compilation and review of space -enabled freshwater projects and their 
alignment with policy priorities, including the identiﬁ cation of  gaps and 
opportunities; 
• A complementary assessment of the integration of space and non -space 
technologies into water policies, and of water considerations into space policies; 
• Insights from stakeholders which, combined with other collected data, inform the 
identiﬁcation of strategic patterns and opportunities for space and water 
collaboration, as well as actionable recommendations.  
1.2 Scope and structure of the study 
The study considers blue water (found in rivers, lakes, groundwater, aquifers) and green 
water (held in soil moisture, taken up by plants), accounting for both visible and often-
overlooked components of the water cycle. 9 It covers all space applications contributing 
to water monitoring : Meteorology, Earth observation (EO), Positioning, Navigation & 
Timing (PNT), Satellite communications (Satcom).  
The analysis spans 32 countries, selected based on their membership in – or affiliation with 
– the European Space Agency (ESA) , the European Union (EU) , and/or the European 
Economic Area (EEA).10 This broader framework extends beyond the EU policy ecosystem 
while encompassing all European countries with a space policy (see Annex B).

1.3 Methodology  
Consistent with the study’s objectives, the analysis required a screening of sectorial policies 
and projects, primarily conducted through desk research combined with data science tools, 
and complemented by a stakeholder  consultation process consisting of multiple expert 
interviews to help shape and validate preliminary ﬁndings, identify patterns and mapping 
preliminary results against real life practice (See Figure 2). 
 
Figure 2 – Overview of methodology steps 
30 freshwater-related policy objectives (‘use cases’) were identiﬁed at the national level, 
relying on policy and strategy documents with a clear and general focus on freshwater, 
excluding those focusing on one single use case (e.g., heavy precipitations) and those older 
than 2016 (unless being the only available national water policy). Using data science tools, 
a complementary assessment was conducted on the extent to which space and non-space 
technologies are integrated in water policies, as well as how water is integrated in space 
policies. 
In terms of space applications, more than 250 ongoing and planned projects* addressing 
freshwater use cases using  space technologies were selected (EO, PNT and Satcom) , 
including those integrated with non -space technologies (e.g., drones).  Details on the 
project selection methodology and their national distribution can be found in Annex B. It 
should be noted that  the comprehensiveness of project collection  is limited by the 
accessibility of project information online, and that the deﬁnition of “project”  adopted in 
this report focuses on downstream solutions.

*Project – A funded initiative focused on the research, development, testing, 
deployment, or scaling of space -based solutions and services  for a speciﬁc 
objective. It encompasses four different output types:  
Programme – A wider initiative involving several  operational activities and (often 
longer-term) objectives, extending beyond single technical outputs.  
System/Platform – Final output is a deployed, operational system or application 
(often online) translating space data into a speciﬁc service, tool or product.  
Pilot Initiative – Research projects that have moved beyond the theoretical phase and 
are actively validating/testing solutions or services in a simulated or real -world 
environment, including operational pilots. 
Research/Feasibility – Time-limited initiatives focused on early-stage research, with 
the primary output being theoretical knowledge, a feasibility study, or a research 
paper.  
Building on a new  methodological approach compared to previous ESPI research , each 
project has been assigned an ‘impact magnitude score’ to determine a project’s weight by 
measuring its size and capacity to be sustainably funded with long- lasting impact (See 
Annex B). This assessment was used to  compare the main objectives  (i.e. use cases 
addressed) of the project to the level of policy prioritisation of the same use cases in 
national water policies, leading to the identiﬁcation of gaps and potential opportunities. 
This accounts for the fact that some apparent ‘gaps’ in comparing the level of policy 
prioritisation and number /size of projects reﬂect limited applicability of space-based 
solutions rather than unmet potential for additional beneﬁts. The analysis of the technical 
feasibility of space solutions which could ﬁll existing implementation gaps is beyond the 
scope of this study. 
Finally, strategic patterns were shaped in a sense- making exercise by combining data-
derived observations from the policy analysis with insights from collected space projects 
and the stakeholder consultation. They provide a more comprehensive basis for actionable 
recommendations for space and water policy alignment.  
Insights from the ESPI Vienna Space Diplomats (VSD) Evening Event on Space for Water 
Diplomacy11 have also signiﬁcantly contributed to shaping  and validating initial results. 
The detailed methodology can be found in Annex B to this report.

2 THE SPACE-WATER POLICY NEXUS 
Increasing alignment between water and space policy agendas  begins with establishing 
an overarching understanding of the needs identiﬁed by countries and whether space plays 
a role in their policy and operational responses. This section includes an analysis of which 
freshwater priorities are reﬂected in water and space national policies  (§2.1) and the 
extent to which they are integrated in  space applications  (§2.2). Comparing policy 
priorities with the current landscape of water -related space projects  enables the 
identiﬁcation of areas of alignment, opportunities and remaining gaps (§2.3). 
2.1 National Water Policy Priorities 
The use cases were  identiﬁed through a qualitative screening of sectorial policies, 
strategies and regulations with a clear relevance to freshwater – water policies, but also 
climate resilience and agriculture policies – at international, European and national levels, 
published primarily between 2016 and 2025 (older policies were excluded, unless being the 
only available national water policy - see Annex B for the full use case description).

2.2 Space and Water Policy Integration  
Integrating water and space policy agendas requires a two -fold approach: (1) 
understanding whether space applications are recognised as tools for addressing national 
water policy priorities, and (2) whether water is framed as an application domain in 
national space agendas.  References to non-space technologies, such as IoT , in water 
policies also indicates a broader appetite for data -driven solutions beyond space 
applications.  This section examines the integration of space and non-space technologies 
in national water policies, of water considerations in national space policies, and compares 
the results.  
2.2.1  Space and Non-Space Technologies Integration in Water Policies 
Freshwater policies demonstrate s igniﬁcant integration of space and non-space 
technologies, with a quarter of all countries referencing both. Nearly  60% of screened 
countries made references to space technologies and applications in their freshwater -
related policy documents, while around 45% referenced non-space technologies with  
references distributed evenly across Europe. (See Figure 5) 
Around 20% of countries refer to neither space nor non-space technologies in the screened 
policies. These include France and Italy, despite managing the largest share of space 
projects together with Germany and the UK (see §2.3.1). 
In some countries, results show a signiﬁcant policy appetite for solutions involving non-
space technologies, but a lack of awareness of existing or potential space solutions. In 
fact, nearly 20% of countries reference non-space technologies – broadly (e.g., “emerging 
technologies”), or more speciﬁcally (e.g., “IoT”) – without mentioning space. This shows a 
potential awareness gap for certain use cases, such as drinking water production. 
Urban wastewater collection and treatment appears the most (nearly 20%) in non-space 
technologies references, followed by r eduction of water losses , surface water pollution 
management and inland ﬂood risk management. (See all use cases in Figure 14, Annex B). 
National trends 
Spain has the highest number of mentions of both space and non-space technologies, 
followed by Cyprus  and Austria for space mentions. National water policies vary 
considerably in length, but this alone does not explain differences in (space) 
technologies integration. Longer documents may allow for more technological 
references through greater operational detail (e.g., Spain, Austria), yet several lengthy 
policies contain few or no ne (e.g., France, Italy, Sweden, Liechtenstein). Conversely, 
Cyprus and Slovenia stand out for their numerous space mentions despite relatively 
short documents.

2.3 Landscape of Existing Water-Related Space Projects  
While policy integration encourages the adoption of space- based solutions and the 
allocation of resources, it does not guarantee practical implementation. It is therefore 
crucial to examine the extent to which national priorities translate into operational space-
based projects. This section provides an overview of the primary statistics of the projects 
collected, followed by a deep dive into  their technical foundations and the water policy 
priorities they address.

applications for freshwater priorities rely overwhelmingly on Earth observation data (95%). 
While most operate at a large scale, a signiﬁcant number of projects are implemented at 
the local level, reﬂecting the site-speciﬁc nature of water management.  Furthermore, 
achieving maximum i mpact often requires combining space data with  complementary 
inputs, with only one in ﬁve projects reliant on the integration of non-space technologies, 
particularly AI and Machine Learning, to translate space data into operational services. An 
overview of the projects collected is shown in Figure 8 below, providing the main statistics

Notably, a disconnect can be observed between national policy integration and project 
implementation in the space-water sector. Italy, France, Germany, and the UK manage the 
largest share of water-related space projects, despite having very limited cross-references 
between space and water in their national policies (See §2.2.1 & §2.2.2). Conversely, policy 
focus does not guarantee implementation . For instance, Cyprus heavily cross- references 
both sectors in its national policies, ranking alongside the Netherlands, Germany, Spain, 
and Austria, yet manages very few active projects , whether through the participation of 
national authorities and ministries in consortia or through companies headquartered in the 
country. While such gaps can also emerge due to differences in national space capabilities 
and industrial policy frameworks, this highlights the need for increased alignment between 
the integration of space and water in policies with concrete project implementation.  
2.3.2 Current and Emerging Data Sources and Technologies 
In terms of space technologies used, 94% of analysed space projects are enabled by Earth 
Observation, 4% combine EO with GNSS and 2% use solely GNSS, while a few also leverage 
meteorological data. Satellite communication primarily supports ﬁeld data transmission 
from remote areas without internet connectivity (e.g., from remote water pipelines ) and 
rarely appears as a project’s main space technology used. GNSS-enabled projects, while 
in minority, draw on speciﬁc sensing methods for geohazards monitoring, such as GNSS 
Reﬂectometry (GNSS-R) with applications focused on ocean, ice and land monitoring. It is 
the technology used for instance by ESA’s recent mission HydroGNSS, which measures soil 
moisture, wetland inundation and freeze-thaw states, among other hydrological variables. 
Beyond the monitoring of  geophysical parameters, GNSS-enabled projects also address 
operational use cases such as water leak detection and water quality monitoring.   
Satellites from the Sentinel and Landsat constellations constitute a major share of the data 
sources used by  space projects , alongside Prisma and historical data from ERS -2 
(European Remote-Sensing Satellite-2). The resulting applications primarily rely on data 
acquired through multispectral optical sensors, synthetic aperture radar ( SAR), thermal 
infrared sensors, and microwave radiometers.

Overall, contrasted alignment is observed between policy objectives and active space-
based projects. While there generally tends to be fewer related projects where there is less 
policy focus, use cases highly addressed by policies are not always represented by an 
equivalent level of implementation through space projects , taking into account the type, 
size, and impact magnitude of these projects.  
Particularly strong alignment is visible in the hydrometeorological risk monitoring & 
management cluster. However, since these applications act more as underlying drivers 
than standalone use cases, they should not be evaluated in isolation. Their high prevalence 
reﬂects how deeply interlinked they are with other use cases (e.g. drought directly reduces 
water availability), while also being tied to the fact that these hydrometeorological risks – 
in particular ﬂoods and droughts – are commonly covered by insurance products, creating 
demand for  technology-driven solutions that can  monitor, predict  and quantify their 
frequency, severity and characteristics.14  
More speciﬁc opportunities can be identiﬁed in the following use cases:   
• Urban water management –  Use cases within this category show particularly low 
operational implementation. Despite being prioritised by 72% of countries , urban 
wastewater collection & treatment remains under-addressed by space projects. While 
expert consultations revealed that its  operational and localised nature makes space 
applications less obvious, remote sensing can help to identify suitable sites for 
wastewater infrastructure while protecting surrounding water sources. There is 
similarly low alignment for urban rainwater collection and reuse, despite the capacity 
of space technologies to detect and combine vital indicators such as soil moisture, 
elevation, and slope.15 Adapting these applications to urban contexts by integrating 
space-based indicators with in-situ data would support municipalities in optimising 
both rainwater collection planning and reuse (e.g. urban green space irrigation). 
• Water infrastructure resilience –  Sustainable water supply management, water -
sensitive urban design, and drinking water production are high policy priorities that 
remain under-addressed by space projects. Stakeholder consultations highlighted that 
since water supply and sanitation are considered ground-level operations, the value 
of space data is often overlooked. However, existing projects demonstrate how space 
technologies are already being leveraged to optimise pumping schedules during low-
ﬂow periods , monitor reservoirs, and enable predictive maintenance across pipe 
networks. Beyond traditional utilities, space data also supports water-sensitive urban 
design, such as the development of sponge cities, by guiding blue-green infrastructure 
placement to strengthen ﬂood resilience.  Scaling these applications is key to 
maximising their impact.

3 CONVERGING CURRENTS: STRATEGIC PATTERNS AT THE 
SPACE-WATER INTERFACE 
Beyond the opportunities identiﬁed through the policy and project analysis, an additional 
layer of analysis incorporating insights from stakeholder consultations was conducted to 
capture the broader dynamics  and emerging patterns at the intersection of space and 
data-driven water policy implementation. This section explores the key dimensions along 
which these strategic patterns emerge: 
 
1. CONNECTING WATER GOVERNANCE AND SPACE SOLUTIONS
The interaction between water governance and space ecosystems is shaped by
contrasting institutional logics revealing three interrelated trends: (1) different
governance scales (decentralised water governance framework vs. centralised
scale of space ecosystem); (2) a selective involvement of water governance actors
in space solution design despite their prominent role as end-users; and (3) multiple
but fragmentedp athwa ysfor dialogue between the space and water sectors.
4. SPACE AS AN ENABLER OF TRANSBOUNDARY WATER COOPERATION 
Space is already supporting transboundary water cooperation through shared and
independently verifiable data, yet water diplomacy remains largely absent from
space policies and is rarely framed as an explicit objective for space solutions. As
space data helps reduce information asymmetries and build trust between riparian
countries, its potential extends beyond monitoring to actively supporting conflict
prevention and resolution, as well as regional water security.
3. EXPANDING THE FINANCING BASE FOR SPACE AND WATER 
Water-related space solutions have the potential to draw on a broader financing
ecosystem, with private companies and International Financial Institutions (IFIs)
financing open-access platforms, investing in digital solutions and helping create
future demand for space solutions and services. However, recognising the extent to
which space data helps, and where complementary in situ data is needed, remains
crucial to avoiding misdirected investments.
2. PRIVATE WATER COMPANIES: COMPLEMENTARY BUT DISTINCT PARTNERS 
Private water companies complement projects led by public water governance
actors, but follow distinct priorities: private and public actors rarely appear
together in the same project consortium. Additionally, private water companies
tend to focus on funding targeted and early-stage solutions and seem to help
space projects reach an additional layer of private users.

3.1 Connecting Water Governance and Space Solutions  
The relationship between water governance and the space ecosystem is inﬂuenced by 
contrasting institutional logics  which reveal three interrelated trends: the mismatch 
between the predominantly local and decentralised nature of water governance  and the 
more centralised scale of the space ecosystem; the limited co-creation of space solutions 
with water governance actors despite their prominent role as end-users; and the multiple 
points of connection through which water and space actors currently engage. 
The Local Water Sector Meets a Centralised Space Ecosystem 
Unlike other sectors such as Energy, the water domain  is highly decentralised . 
Implementation largely occurs at the local level, which can limit economies of scale and 
reduce the ﬁnancial and technical capacity of water authorities. Governance is further 
fragmented between water supply and sanitation, typically managed by local utilities at 
the municipal level (this varies signiﬁcantly across European countries), and water resource 
management, to a large extent organised at river  basin level under EU legislation  and 
supported by  governmental oversight , which  usually includes the relevant national 
ministries and/or environmental public agencies.  Therefore, effective implementation 
depends on coordination between local,  national and European governance bodies, an 
important consideration to design effective and tailored space 
solutions addressing water policy priorities.  
While water resource management  and water supply and 
sanitation are primarily managed  at the local level, the 
analysed space projects are mainly deployed globally, 
regionally, or nationally ( see Figure 10). 19% of the analysed 
projects are local in scale, compared to 35% global. However, it 
is worth noting that , while local-scale projects remain least 
represented in comparison to the other scales, the share of 
local-scale projects is signiﬁcantly higher than in the broader 
domains analysed using a similar approach in ESPI’s previous 
research, Where Sensing Makes Sense 20, where the domain with the highest percentage of 
local space projects was Climate Resilience with 12%. This reﬂects the local nature of 
solutions and the importance of in-situ data in the water domain, with ground veriﬁcation 
and the involvement of local stakeholders playing a signiﬁcant role. Conversely, the 
prevalence of projects with a geographical scope transcending the local and national level 
reﬂects the transboundary nature of water resources (See §3.4).

their primary end -users. Only 15% of  the analysed space projects

monitoring or other applications have been made  widely available thanks to this initial

3.4 Space as an Enabler of Transboundary Water Cooperation  
Water is ultimately transboundary in nature and not conﬁned 
to political boundaries ,  a s  r e ﬂ e c t e d  i n  t h e prevalence of 
analysed projects with a geographical scope transcending the 
local and national level.  Nearly 60% of the collected space 
projects have an i nternational impact (i.e. they impact 
multiple countries one more than one continent, see Figure 
13), which is signiﬁcantly higher than the regional impact  in

However, water diplomacy remains a high -level policy 
concept that has  yet to be framed as a concrete objective  
that space solutions can help achieve , both at policy and 
implementation levels. Very few space projects speciﬁcally 
address water diplomacy use cases – namely Enhancing international collaboration in 
business opportunities  and Cooperation on transboundary water basins  (See Figure 9,

international cooperation on shared water resources.  Consulted stakeholders have 
highlighted that, in many transboundary contexts, negotiations are hindered by either the 
absence of data, outdated data, or disagreements over the credibility of datasets.  In this 
regard, by providing common and independently veriﬁable information across borders, 
space-based data facilitate the management of transboundary river basins while also 
creating the conditions for informed discussions and greater trust between riparian states.

diplomacy use cases, the analysis reveals a high degree of co-occurrence between water 
diplomacy and other core use cases. Projects addressing water diplomacy often link 
transboundary cooperation with surface water quality and quantity monitoring, 
hydrometeorological risk management, and energy infrastructure planning. This area 
represents a key opportunity for space technologies to transition from monitoring 
applications towards becoming an active tool for transboundary conﬂict prevention, joint 
resource planning, and regional water security objectives.

4 RECOMMENDATIONS  
The analysis and stakeholder consultation process demonstrate that the uptake of space 
technologies in the water sector depends on addressing gaps in governance, collaboration 
and policy integration. The recommendations below focus on strengthening the connection 
between space and water sector needs, priorities and capabilities, ensuring that solutions 
are designed, implemented and scaled in response to operational challenges and through 
a common agenda. 
4.1 Positioning Space as a Core Component of Water Management Solutions 
Finding | Awareness of space applications within the water sector is relatively high: nearly 
60% of countries analysed refer to space technologies in their water policy documents, while 
only one ﬁfth of all countries discuss non-space technologies (IoT, AI/ML, drones) without 
mentioning space. However, this policy awareness does not consistently translate into 
implementation. Countries managing the largest share of water -related space projects  
show limited cross-referencing between space and water in their national policies, while 
others extensively reference both sectors but implement comparatively few projects. This 
suggests that water and space policies evolve in parallel rather than reinforcing one 
another: water policies prioritise primarily water resource management and water quality, 
while space policies tend to prioritise hydrometeorological risks, i.e. established use cases 
where the value of space is already demonstrated (e.g., droughts and inland ﬂooding).  
Recommendation | To encourage the development of solutions that directly address 
existing water policy challenges and improve the translation of policy priorities into 
space projects, there is a need to align cross- references between space and water 
policies around shared priority areas, where relevant. National space policies should 
explicitly recognise water as a strategic application area, while in countries promoting 
technology-enabled water solutions without reference to space, strengthening space 
considerations in water policies can help create entry points for the development of 
satellite-based solutions aligned by design with sectoral priorities. 
4.2 Co-designing Space Solutions with Water Sector Actors  
Finding | Active co-creation of space solutions for water applications remains limited: only 
slightly over 25% of the projects collected include a water sector actor in their consortium, 
public or private. Public water actors especially, such as municipalities and public agencies, 
make up nearly half of the primary end -users but remain absent from 85% of the project 
consortia, risking a disconnect between technology providers and implementation. Projects 
including private water actors in their consortium not only reach public entities but also an 
additional set of private end -users, including hydropower producers, water supply 
companies and small-holder farmers.  

--- Page 38 ---
Converging Currents - Recommendations to Align Space & Water Policy Agendas in Europe 
 
European Space Policy Institute (ESPI)  36 
 
Recommendation | Public and private water actors should be systematically involved 
as co-designers of water-related space projects rather than only consultees. Involving 
end-users throughout project development would strengthen trust, operational 
relevance and buy-in of solutions. Successful examples in Europe and Africa already 
exist, including ﬂood mapping initiatives bringing together space agenc ies, 
hydrological services, meteorological agenc ies and ministries to develop solutions 
tailored to national and regional needs. 
4.3 Connecting Space Solutions with Water Governance Frameworks through 
Local Actors  
Finding | Unlike the space sector, the water sector operates through a decentralised 
governance structure, with implementation largely taking place at the basin and municipal 
level. Accordingly, collected local-scale projects, are more prevalent in the water domain 
than in other sectors, even though nearly 60% of analysed projects operate at the continent 
or global scale . While global and open -access platforms provide common tools and 
datasets, local actors often require tailored applications that reﬂect speciﬁc governance 
arrangements (e.g., river basin authorities) and water management challenges (e.g., 
wetland health monitoring). Consultations also revealed that stakeholder mapping is rarely 
funded as part of project design, limiting the understanding of the institutional landscape 
and the needs space solutions are expected to address.  
Recommendation | Space solutions should be designed around  the decentralised  
nature of  water governance frameworks, informed by funded and mandatory 
stakeholder mapping. Systematically identifying governance actors, responsibilities 
and decision-making processes would allow solutions to be adapted to the water 
sector policy priorities and operational needs rather than importing external 
assumptions, therefore increasing their relevance and adoption. To enable this 
process, the sustained involvement of local and regional water actors  – such as 
country offices of international organisations , international conventions, 
hydrometeorological organisations, disaster management services, NGOs – is 
essential. These actors should increasingly act as intermediaries between end users 
and providers of space solutions to help translate water sector needs into tailored 
solutions that effectively support decision-making.

4.4 Coordinating Action at the River Basin Level  
Finding | River basin organisations remain largely absent from the analysed space projects, 
despite being uniquely positioned to support national and cross-border water 
management. The prevalence of space projects with a geographical scope transcending 
the local and national level reﬂects the transboundary nature of water and the diversity of 
actors needed to develop relevant transboundary solutions. As long-standing international 
institutions, river basin organisations possess valuable governance experience and GIS/HIS 
datasets, especially in Europe due to the presence of numerous ground monitoring stations. 
In certain cases, but not systematically,  these data are integrated with satellite- based 
meteorological data mainly for ﬂood and drought forecasting. However, this integration 
necessitates building the capacities of river basin organisations in a context of often 
constrained resources, with peer learning as a valuable way to disseminate lessons learned 
across basins meeting different challenges. Further integration of satellite data with these 
organisations’ ground observations would strengthen the work of river basin organisations 
beyond ﬂood and drought forecasting , towards freshwater ecosystem management and 
water quality. 
Recommendation | Building on existing networks and peer-learning initiatives 
between river basin organisations and space actors,38 communities of practice should 
evolve to help position river basin organisations as co-designers of space projects for 
water management . Dedicated, frequent and sustained dialogue should happen 
between river basin organisations and space solutions providers, with a focus on 
needs identiﬁcation and capacity building . Beyond knowledge exchange, this 
sustained dialogue holds the potential to move from  ad hoc collaboration towards 
shaping a shared agenda  between space and river basin management actors,  
allowing to further integrate space technologies into ( transboundary) water basin 
governance.

Finding |  Recent water policies (2019-2025) place growing emphasis on water security 
considerations, which covers water availability, water infrastructure resilience and water 
diplomacy. While the water security cluster remains less prioritised overall, water 
infrastructure resilience is a major policy priority, encompassing sustainable water supply 
management, water -sensitive urban design, and drinking water production. Yet, this 
priority remains under-addressed by space projects. Stakeholder s highlighted how the 
value of space data is often overlooked in water supply and sanitation, traditionally viewed

4.6 Leveraging Private Actors and Finance  
Finding |  Private water actors (e.g., private water suppliers, water management 
consultancies) are involved in only 20% of the space projects analysed , with little overlap 
with public water actors. Yet, in addition to municipalities and public agencies, they 
provide access to private end -users (e.g., insurers, large producers), some of which are 
well-positioned to help design and ﬁnance space solutions. Stakeholder consultations 
highlighted that private companies relying on water for their activities also have an interest 
in reducing their environmental footprint and supporting river basin ecosystem 
preservation and restoration. Private companies also hold an active role in sponsoring 
publicly-developed, open-access space-based platforms (e.g., for hydrological modelling), 
while indirectly stimulating future demand for space solutions.  
Recommendation | Opportunities to co -ﬁnance water-related space solutions with 
private companies, and to increase the participation of private water actors in space 
projects consortia, should be actively explored. Such partnerships would help broaden 
the reach of space solutions and scale up applications addressing operational needs 
and preventive action (e.g., leak detection, water supply management).

4.7 Recognising the Limits of Space Technologies and Maximising their Added 
Value  
Finding | The ﬁndings highlight the importance of avoiding an overly technology -driven 
approach to freshwater management. S takeholder consultations emphasised the risk of 
“overselling” the potential of  the contribution of space technologies to freshwater 
applications, which can lead to misdirected investment. They consistently underscored how 
space data, such as EO-derived observations, deliver the greatest value when combined 
with in-situ data and embedded within broader governance frameworks.  
Consultations further underscored that efficiency gains from digital technologies, including 
space technologies, do not automatically translate into water conservation and, in some 
cases, can lead to the opposite effect. This reﬂects the Jevons Paradox, which describes 
how an increase in the utilisation efficiency of a resource (usually caused by technological 
advancements) creates a reduction in price, subsequently generating a higher demand and 
usage for that resource ( rebound effect).39 In agriculture, tools enabling more efficient 
water use often lead to the expansion of the cultivated area instead of reducing overall 
water consumption. This risk is exacerbated in countries where water allocations remain 
ﬁxed and low abstraction charges provide little incentive to reduce consumption, with such 
decisions often entrusted to regional/state governments, in application of the principle of 
subsidiarity.40  
Recommendation | Space-based services should be framed as complements to, rather 
than substitutes for, in-situ data and local expertise. To this end, Earth observation 
data standardisation efforts should not evolve in complete isolation from in-situ data 
management practices . Instead, integrating space - and ground -based data will 
facilitate their mutual adoption by both space and water management communities. 
At the same time, investments in space-enabled water solutions should be paired with 
appropriate national policy reforms, notably regarding water allocation frameworks, 
pricing mechanisms and maximum abstraction limits, to ensure that efficiency gains 
result in genuine improvements in freshwater management rather than expanded 
water use.

4.8 The way forward 
The outcomes of this study aim to inform ongoing and future policy processes at the 
design, review and implementation stages . While this study expanded its geographical 
scope beyond EU Member States, ongoing EU initiatives provide short- and medium-term 
opportunities to institutionalise the space-water dialogue, building on the sectors’ existing 
awareness of each other’s activities.  
The implementation of the European Water Resilience Strategy41 – including its Research 
and Innovation agenda – offers a natural entry point for integrating Earth observation and 
other space-based services into water policy development and implementation. Notably, 
the future Copernicus Wat er Hub has the potential to become a n important enabler  for 
space & water priorities alignment, as it will become a common entry point for water 
stakeholders and connect their needs with existing space capabilities. Its impact could be 
reinforced through capacity-building,42 as well as the improvement of interoperability, data 
sharing and user access through the development of national water data portals.43  
Beyond Europe, international policy processes,  including the UN Water Conference, also 
provide opportunities to strengthen the visibility of space technologies as practical tools 
for freshwater management and cooperation over shared waters. They hold the potential 
to evolve into recurring gatherings, supported by  structured preparation processes 
involving relevant stakeholders at the regional  level (e.g., municipalities, NGOs, private 
water actors, space solutions providers) to develop concrete programmes and initiatives, 
building on existing outcomes of water conferences (e.g., UN Water Conference ﬂagship 
initiatives). The conferences would then provide an opportunity to assess the advancement 
of the commitment of countries , both at the policy level and in terms of operational 
implementation, including the uptake of technological innovations.

Annex B – Detailed Methodology 
Water and Space Policy Analysis 
The document selection follows a structured and multi -level approach to ensure both 
relevance and comparability across sources and countries. To have a comprehensive 
understanding of the way freshwater is addressed at policy level, freshwater use cases 
were identiﬁed based on the qualitative screening of the following documents: 
• Reports from the  Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change  (IPCC AR6 WGII Full 
Report; IPCC AR6 WGIII Full Report; IPCC AR6 SYR Longer Report) relevant to the topic 
of water;  
• National Strategic Plans on the Common Agricultural policy (CAP)  from selected EU 
Countries, chosen for their weight in the total agricultural production and export in the 
Union and geographically distributed across the continent , with a f ocus on section 
2.1SO5; 
• Sections on v ulnerability, climate impacts and adaptation measures from National 
Communications (NCs) under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change;  
• National Adaptation Plans (NAPs) on climate change; 
• For each country national water policies and regulations were also selected. Priority 
was given to official and up-to-date policy documents (incl. strategies, actions plans, 
implementation reports) with a clear relevance to freshwate r. The selection also 
included at least one nation-wide water-related law or regulation per country. The 
national policies were excluded if they were outdated (older than 2016, unless it is the 
only existing water -speciﬁc nation -wide policy), out of scope (e.g., about marine 
environment) or not a nation- wide policy document (e.g., one-off project or service 
utility regulation);  
The national water policies were then analysed to identify policy priorities in the ﬁeld of 
freshwater, later used in the opportunity and gap analysis . When national water policies 
identiﬁed concrete measures to address a speciﬁc use case, this use case was then counted 
as a policy priority.  All non-English documents have been translated in English using the 
platform DeepL, to avoid differences in interpretation.  
Quantitative analysis 
In order to assess the level of mutual integration in national space and water policies , a 
quantitative analysis was conducted on a total of nearly 100 policy documents by running, 
on the one hand, a dictionary of space-related words as well as a dictionary of non-space 
technologies words on water policies, and on the other hand a dictionary of water-related 
words on space policies. The results from the automated screening have been checked 
manually to eliminate irrelevant or double mentions.

Level of Implementation  
The level of implementation assesses the extent to which space projects currently address 
policy priorities across identiﬁed use cases. This extends beyond the absolute number of 
projects addressing a given priority to also account for each project’s size, scale, and 
potential for long-term impact.  
To deﬁne the level of implementation, an “impact magnitude” score is determined for each 
project, p lacing greater weight on initiatives with strong institutional backing and 
sustainable funding over small -scale projects with limited funding , though these can be 
highly impactful at the local level. These individual scores are then aggregated to establish 
an overall level of implementation (on a three- tier scale of Low -Medium-High) for the 
gap analysis to identify how well policy priorities align with space project implementation. 
It should be noted that this weighting method was only used for the gap analysis and does 
not inﬂuence the assessment of other project characteristics, such as scale  of 
implementation, impact region, or funding type.  
To determine the impact magnitude of a space- based project, a 
branching (decision tree) approach is taken with a scoring system of 1 
(lowest) to 5 (highest). A score of 5 is not simply ﬁve times a score of 1; 
the steps are deliberately non -linear. The assessment begins with a 
categorisation of the “output type”. A maximum score is deﬁned for 
each output type according to the highest potential impact magnitude 
that each output type can achieve.  
Across all analysed projects, a score of 2 was the most common impact 
magnitude (45%), representing early -stage initiatives across all output 
types excluding programmes. Projects scoring 4  made up 23% of all 
projects and consisted entirely of systems/platforms, many of which are 
commercial platforms with institutional consortia stakeholders. Projects 
receiving a 3 (21%) were represented by pilot initiatives and commercial 
systems/platforms. Scores at the two extremes were less common, with 9% scoring 5, and 
just 2% scoring 1. All programmes received a score of 5 due to their large budgets and 
involvement of institutional stakeholders. Conversely, level 1 projects were much smaller in 
scale, with limited funding, smaller consortia, and purely research-oriented objectives.

Criteria 
• Budget size – Budget tends to be the primary factor inﬂuencing a project’s reach and

○ This is a fast-track criterion for all types, with a speciﬁc threshold deﬁned for each.

• Own website – Whether a project has an established website is often an indicator of

• Stakeholders Involved – This refers to the type of entities involved in the consortium. 
In this assessment, Institutional/High-Level Stakeholders (e.g. Ministries, government 
entities, big public agencies, space agencies, large international organisations, UN 
agencies, big companies) hold the most weight. Private actors are divided into big 
companies (e.g. ICEYE) and small startups. Small startups, small 
organisations/institutes, and academia hold the same weight as each other, which is 
assessed as lower than that of the aforementioned entities.  
○ More weight is given to institutional/high-level stakeholders (e.g. government 
entities, UN agencies, space agencies, large companies) because they hold more 
institutional weight and tend to increase the longevity and large-scale capacity of 
the project, therefore leading to a higher score than small-scale private actors and 
academic institutions. 
○ When small UN offices are involved with clear substantial contribution, they can 
also fall under the “institutional stakeholder” category , but this  is assessed on a 
case-by-case basis. 
• The type of beneﬁciaries/end -users – This refers to the target end -users of the 
solution. Following a similar logic to the above description, institutional/high -level 
stakeholders (e.g. Ministries, government entities, large international organisations or 
UN agencies) hold more weight than smaller -scale stakeholders (e.g. startups,

The outliers are projects scoring 1; scoring 1 is the result of strong disqualiﬁers which hinder

scoring 2 is higher than the step factor for the following scores: the gap in impact between 
a score 1 and a score 2 is wider than between a score 2 and 3, 3 and 4, and 4 and 5.
