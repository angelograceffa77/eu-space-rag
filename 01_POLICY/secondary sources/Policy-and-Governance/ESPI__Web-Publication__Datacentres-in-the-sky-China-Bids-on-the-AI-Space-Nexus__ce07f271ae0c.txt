Datacentres in the sky? China Bids on the AI-Space Nexus
Datacentres in the sky? China Bids on the AI-Space Nexus
Published:
11 October 2026
The following article was written by ESPI Roy Gibson Fellow, Lea Thome, and was originally published by
The Wire
on 11 October 2026.
“Let’s take our data centers, put them in Earth orbit, take advantage of that free fusion reactor that’s out there for us and use that to ensure we do win the … space race for sure, but the AI race, too,” said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman in an interview in late August, as U.S.
public opinion
on data centers is rapidly dimming and as demand for computing capacity is rising in the age of advanced artificial intelligence.
While Isaacman’s interview marks a first high-level official endorsement in the U.S. of orbital data centers outside of the private sector, where SpaceX’s Elon Musk has been one of the leading voices of the idea, China has already moved space computing from early exploration to scaling. In a short period of a year, an entire ecosystem dedicated to this nexus of space and AI, two of the most important domains China is strategically pursuing, has been constructed composed of expert groups, private enterprises, municipal initiatives, and research institutions.
Building an early ecosystem: China’s push for space computing
The world’s computing capacity is now experiencing twofold growth every seven months, according to
Epoch AI
. To accommodate this rising demand, industry has started to look at orbital data centres (ODCs). ODCs offer the opportunity to compute and store data in space before sending it back to earth, for applications ranging from earth observation to potential military uses.
As we outline in a
brief for the European Space Policy Institute
, China’s implementation of orbital data centers most notably took off in May last year. At the time, ADAspace and Zhejiang Lab, the former a Chengdu-based company and the latter a Hangzhou-based research lab
funded by the Zhejiang Provincial Government
, launched their Three-Body Computing Constellation. Initially, the two entities
launched
the first twelve satellites of up to “2,400 inference computing satellites and 400 training computing satellites” in low-earth orbit.
Since this moment in May 2025, several companies and initiatives have followed in rapid succession to innovate their space computing constellations. Ahead and on the sidelines of the World Artificial Intelligence Conference held in Shanghai in mid-July, at least three new space computing initiatives have been announced, spearheaded by Shanghai Xingshu Tiansuan Space Technology Co., SenseTime, and Nayuta Space.
Note: This table was compiled by the authors and is based upon research conducted for the European Space Policy Institute (2026).
These announcements come at a time when Chinese companies are moving ahead both on space, with China’s launch capabilities set to strengthen through its breakthrough in rocket recovery, and AI, with Zhipu.AI and DeepSeek releasing highly-capable models. To fuel China’s demand for AI, the Chinese government and other companies have resorted to exploring
underwater data centers
,
terrestrial data centers in remote inner Mongolia
, as well as space-based data centers as alternative options.
Simultaneously, China’s policy development has also kept up with this growing industry momentum. As we predicted in our brief, China also recently adopted the idea of space computing in its national-level documents. China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology
strategized
“building efficient and coordinated computing infrastructure” through “[exploring] forward-looking research and ecosystem development for space-based computing power” in its 15th five-year plan for 2026 to 2030—signalling broader national buy-in for these initiatives.
In a united push to advance market opportunities and feasibly challenges, even typical competitors in China’s space economy got together to establish a Space Computing Industry Innovation Center led by the Beijing Postal and Telecommunications School, the National Information Technology Application Innovation Park, LandSpace, GalaxySpace, as well as other enterprises in April 2026. Outside of Beijing, Shanghai’s Songjiang district held two industry and academic conferences on space computing, ultimately establishing a
Space Computing Industrial Base
within the district.
What does space computing mean for China and the world?
China, of course, is not the only country to bet on space computing. In the U.S., SpaceX already
filed plans
with the Federal Communications Commission in early January to “deploy a system of up to one million satellites”, while Google, NVIDIA, and Blue Origin have proposed similar plans. StarCloud, who recently
raised $250 million U.S. dollars
in a $2.3 billion valuation, already
launched
its StarCloud-1 in late 2025.
Europe, meanwhile, has continued exploring the technology through a Commission workshop and a European Space Agency
request for information
on space-based data centers. European companies like Alatyr have also announced their first constellations, while
Thales Alenia Space
conducted a feasibility study for orbital data centers, “[confirming] the project’s economic viability, offering a prospect of a return on investment of several billion euros between now and 2050.”
This headway in space computing signals Beijing’s understanding that as orbit is becoming increasingly congested, actual space for these massive constellations, ranging from a proposed 1,000 satellites to 1 million in size, in orbit matters. Beijing is also hoping to avoid multiple constraints that it is facing in terrestrial data centres: large-scale energy and water usage to fuel data centres, citizen approval, and geopolitical tensions that shape computing capacity.
Yet there are multiple challenges that China, and others, needs to address first before it can get to these space computing constellations. The science behind space computing remains complex, with researchers and companies still exploring how to power and cool these data centers, while launch costs for computing satellites need to be drastically lowered to become profitable, even with China’s recent advances in rocket reusability.
Similarly, many of the plans of companies like Nayuta and SenseTime remain ambitious, requiring over 300 satellites to be launched per year to meet 2030 targets. ADAspace’s Three-Body Constellation, although hailed as an early space computing success, has not launched any new computing satellites either since its initial May 2025 launch.
And most importantly, to conduct in-orbit computing Beijing needs what it currently has limited access to: advanced chips which the U.S. has imposed export restrictions on. To illustrate, SpaceX
announced
a partnership with NVIDIAs for its ‘Starmind AI1 satellites’ using NVIDIA’s forthcoming Vera Rubin graphics processing unit (GPU) and its Vera central processing unit (CPU).
Ultimately, China is investing in setting up an ecosystem for space computing that may or may not pay off in the short-term. Rather than playing catch-up, however, China is trying to set the tone. Chinese officials, companies, and researchers understand that computing capacity is vital for the world’s technological transformation, just like space.
