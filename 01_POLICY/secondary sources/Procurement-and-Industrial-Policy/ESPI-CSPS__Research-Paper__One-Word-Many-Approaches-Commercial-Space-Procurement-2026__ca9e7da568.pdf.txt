1 One Wor (l) d Many Approaches   
“Commercial” is everywhere in the space sector today, becoming shorthand for aspirations 
of shared abundance, a marker for space program me maturity, and a means  of mission 
assurance through diversity of competition. Encouraged by the expansion of private 
companies creating innovative space solutions, policymakers increasingly use the term to 
define their strategy for bolstering economic growth , expanding national capabilities , and 
improving resilience . This phenomenon is happening across the globe but is es pecially 
prevalent in the United States  and Europe, two traditional space powers attempting to 
maintain  or reestablish  their lead over rising space powers.  
Despite important differences in the evolution of their respective space sectors and 
capabilities, the United States and Europe both recognise  commercial space companies 
as vital to their economic and military competitiveness .1 In th e United States , NASA has 
built sufficient confidence with  its commercial cargo and crew program mes to turn to 
commercial solutions for key components of the Artemis missions  to return to the Moon. 
The United States  Space Force, which  released its first “Commercial Space Strategy” in 
2024,  has greatly increased  its spending on commercial space services and has overhauled 
some critical weapons program mes, prioritising  available commercial solutions . Similarly, 
Europe is driving  multiple efforts at the national and regional level to both en courage and 
commercial ise space innovation. Examples such as  the ESA ’s ScaleUp , 3CPO, and ELC 
programmes  and the EU’s CASSINI  provide  a variety of resources to help foster and 
accelerate  innovative commercial space companies and entrepreneurs.  
Yet across all these announcements , initiatives and new budget lines, an important 
question remains – what exactly does “commercial” mean?  The answer may seem 
obvious, but an examination of commercially -aligned initiatives and missions suggests it 
is both complex and unsettled. Most people associate “commercial” with products or 
services they can purchase on the open market, such as a car or  laptop, where there are 
many buyers and sellers. While this is indeed happening today in some areas of space, 
particu larly for well -established markets such as satellite communications services or 
remote sensing imagery, it hardly describes the whole space ecosystem. Many government 
space initiatives labelled “commercial” seek to develop capabilities that do not currentl y 
exist, such as crewed lunar landers, or for which the government is the only likely customer, 
such as space -based interceptors. This suggests a much wider definition for “commercial” 
than assumed and that the term is sometimes used flexibly to support di fferent policy and 
strategic objectives. In this sense, “commercial” should not be confused with  consumer -
driven markets ; it can also apply within government -anchored markets .

1.1 What are the objectives  of the study?  
The contemporary interpretation  of commercial procurement is deeply related to the ‘New 
Space’ paradigm, in which new companies are developing space capabilities, 
technologies, and systems for both government and commercial customers , taking on a 
significant share of the risk in the development of said solutions . This has brought, among 
other things, innovative public procurement and support mechanisms to the centre of the 
debate around the commercialisation of space. In essence, the question of what the 
government’s role in commercial space was reopened and  is in search of new answers.  
This project aims to provide more clarity to this question of how governments on both sides 
of the Atlantic are leveraging commercial space. It is a joint effort between The Aerospace 
Corporation’s Center for Space Policy and Strategy (CSPS) and the European Space Policy 
Institute (ESPI) , two global leaders in space policy analysis,  that brings together experts  
from both institutions to examine this question of why and how the United States  and 
Europe are leveraging commercial space.  
The project consists of two phases. Phase 1, herein, proposes a framework for 
characterising and classifying government commercial procurement initiatives and 
contractual strategies. It proposes three models with different drivers and characteristics: 
Commercial -Lite, Commercial -Led, and Purely Commercial ; and applies them to a  set of 
example efforts in the United States  and Europe.

1.2 What is in the scope of the study ? 
The participation of private companies in government space programmes is not new  – 
from the very beginning of the Space Age , the United States  and Europe have relied on 
private sector capabilities . However, the more recent developments where governments 
are not only relying on private contractors to perform work but shifting a greater share of 
choices and decisions to the private sector represent a significant change in philosophy . 
With a shift in Western economies throughout the late 1970s, space increasin gly became 
understood as a domain that can not only provide complementary value to activities on 
Earth, but itself drive economic growth and (through the advent of global markets)

empower industrial competitiveness. Several  approaches have been  explored  in this study 
to emphasise  limited government involvement, cost sharing, ownership of intellectual 
property by the commercial partner, top -line requirements, pay -for-performance 
milestones (including company ownership in devising the milestones and associated 
payment) and lower cos ts.2  
In the United States, national space policy has for decades supported the development of 
commercial space capabilities and encouraged government entities to make use of them. 
These include the National Space Policy signed by President Reagan in 19 88 that directed 
government agencies to “purchase commercially available space goods and services to 
the fullest extent wherever possible.” 3 However, implementation of these policies in the 
following decades, particularly for major space program mes, was mixed at best. It was 
not until the success of NASA’s Commercial Orbital Transportation Services (COTS) and the 
follow -on Commercial Resupply Services (CRS)  in the 2000s and 2010s  that there was a 
significant shift in the relationship between  U.S. government agencies and  the private 
sector for civil space activities. On the defence side, the National Geospatial Intelligence 
Agency’s 2015  “Commercial GEOINT  Strategy ” was a strong public signal of a similar shift .4 
These new arrangements were very different from the traditional, heavily regulated 
contracts,  where  the opposite was often the case , such as the government providing 
detailed programme design requirements. 5  
In Europe, the  broader  question of commercialisation is also not as recent as it is commonly 
understood. In January 1980, France submitted a declaration to the ESA Council to transfer 
the “commercial responsibility of manufacturing, marketing and launch operations of 
Ariane” fr om ESA to a newly created company called A rianespace. Looking towards 
downstream markets, in 1981, a programme for the first ESA Remote Sensing Satellite 
(ERS -1) was agreed upon whereby it (i.a.) hoped to “ benefit a number of commerci al 
applications (offshore activities, ship routing, ice surveillance, aid to fisheries etc.). ”6 
Simultaneously, as the French commercial satellite imagery company Spot Image was 
being conceived,  supported by a public programme,  hopes emerged of a future market 
that could uptake between 60.000 and 100.000 images per year at an estimated cost of 
$1.000 to $2.000 per image, with primary users coming from (i.a.) the oil & gas, mining, 
and agricultural industries. 7 Beyond programmatic developments,  the ESA Office of Space 
Commercialisation was also created in 1986 to identify ways of preparing for the 
commercialisation of ESA programmes and services. 8

There is an important  nuance for the risk structure under the commercial procurement 
approach. While industry does share more of the contract risk, the government may still 
bear the consequences of not having a critical capability should the contract fail to deliver. 
For examp le, if the contract is to deliver a critical component for a program me, failure 
could jeopardise  the entire program me’s success and resolving it may require additional 
time that the government does not have.  
To better understand what, in practice, can be considered a Commercial Procurement 
Model, and thus what is in scope of this study, it is useful to start by identifying what clearly 
falls outside this scope. On the European side, the Automated Transfer Vehicl e (ATV) 
developed by ESA to transfer cargo to and from the International Space Station (ISS) 
represents a typical case of a Bespoke Development Model ( full public ownership, cost –
reimbursable contracting, and top –down management ) and  therefore lies beyon d the 
analytical scope of this research. The programme was owned and  funded by ESA, fulfilling 
Europe’s  contribution to the ISS partnership. The contract structure followed a cost -
reimbursable model, with ESA covering all development and production expenses and 
retaining full financial and performance responsibility. As for the contractual relationship, 
the industrial partner, Aerospatiale  (now Airbus D&S) , acted as prime contractor executing 
ESA technical directions, under a scheme where decision –making re mained with the 
Agency. The customer base was governmental, and funding came entirely from public 
budgets, with no risk -sharing mechanisms. In terms of capability development, ESA 
defined both the objectives and the technical requirements, leaving marginal  scope for 
industrial discretion or innovation. Finally, the cost & risk structure  was wholly public, 
confirming the absence of commercial elements in the programme design and execution. 13 
On the U.S. side, an example of the Bespoke Development Model is the Global Positioning 
System (GPS) III program, which began in the early 2000s to both sustain the operational 
GPS constellation and deliver new capabilities. Following competition, the U.S.  Air Force 
awarded the contract for GPS III to Lockheed Martin, a traditional contractor that had built 
prior-generation  GPS satellites. The Air Force used cost reimbursement contracts to fund 
GPS III research and development  (R&D),  as well as most procurement activities. Because 
GPS signals are available for commercial use at no charge, the government functions as 
the sole customer for GPS III. Similarly, the government defined the program’s objectives 
and technical requirements, an d the cost and risk structure was wholly public.

2 CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK  
While the aforementioned  definition of commercial procurement  is sufficient to separate it 
from more established  bespoke approaches, it does not clearly identify which mechanisms 
qualify as commercial procurement or capture the nuances of European and U.S. 
ecosystems. To address this, we further expand the init ial definition into  a spectrum -based 
framework that reflects the distinctive features of each ecosystem and recognises that 
individual program mes may combine multiple models. For instance, a programme  may 
align mainly with the Commercial Procurement Model while incorporating elements of the 
Bespoke Development Model; however, if its dominant characteristics reflect the latter, it 
is not included in this analysis.  
2.1 How to define and address  a Commercial Procurement Model?  
Figure 2 demonstrates how we further  divide the Commercial Procurement Model into three 
main categories or approaches with different programme characteristics. The reasoning 
behind each approach ( Commercial -Lite, Commercial -Led, and Purely Commerc ial) was 
informed by relevant documents (e.g. the United States  Federal Acquisition Regulation , ESA 
Contracting Regulation 2025 ) and by identifying gaps in the Bespoke -Commercial 
paradigm across European and United States  procurement programmes. Procurement 
program mes often  reflect more than  one approach , and are most likely to be multi -
dimensional, leaning more “purely commercial” on some characteristics, while being more 
in line with “commercial -Lite” on other characteristics. Nevertheless, overall,  the more a 
programme characteristic leans into supporting market -based dynamics , attributing 
ownership of the product and/or service design, build, financing and operation, to the 
private enterprise, and thus transferring associated risks away from the government , the 
more purely commercial the programme will be.  We note that under these definitions, there 
are very few current space procurement efforts that would fall under the purely commercial 
approach.

Although some of the programme characteristics are self -explanatory, there are still some 
important considerations  that are explored below.  
In the Commercial -Lite Approach,  industry builds and/or operates 
government infrastructure or delivers exclusive services under government 
direction. This role enables the company to scale capabilities and to later offer  its own  
derivative or related products to the broader market. In the Commercial -Led Approach, 
the company owns its product or service from the outset, while the government provides 
specific enablers that reduce development risk. Typical enablers include industry access to 
infrastructure, fa cilities, and data, targeted technology transfer, spectrum access, and 
milestone -based funding. In a Purely Commercial Approach, the industry has full 
ownership over its solutions/products without government intervention.  
The contra ct type is a good indicator to assess the level of risk transfer to 
the private enterprise. The Commercial -Lite Approach  combines a fixed 
price for part of the work with the reimbursement of specific, allowable costs for other 
elements of the work.  The Commercial -Led Approach relies on fixed -price contracts with 
adjustable prices , meaning the private firm  bears a substantial part of the risk but is still 
shielded from  external pressures  because it allows for adjustments based on predefined 
criteri a, such as fluctuations in labour or material costs.  In the Purely Commercial 
Approach, the firm  bears all the risk because the price of the contract  cannot be altered  or 
revised based on the actual costs incurred by the company  in fulfilling its business 
obligations .14

Contract management hinges  on two main concepts:  i) type and quantity 
of firms contracted (e.g., multiple primes ), and ii) what framework 
governs the government/industry relationship.  
The degree of openness in the selection process largely determines the structure of the 
supplier base and signals the government’s intended relationship with industry. When the 
priority is short -term value for money or the delivery of a bespoke project, th e government 
will typically select a single prime contractor. A sole integrator can capture economies of 
scale more effectively than a fragmented set of providers and is more likely to field mature, 
ready -to-apply technologies. By contrast, if the aim is t o stimulate market development in 
the space sector, the process should be open and competitive, replicat ing market dynamics  
by selecting multiple firms and/or ensuring competition in future contract awards . In that 
setting, the government may play a broader role – as investor, technical adviser, and 
partner – helping develop solutions and bring them to market. 15 
These choices also shape the Public -Private Partnership  (PPP) design , when there is one.  
Two considerations are central here. First,  is the level of risk transfer to the private entity, 
which depends, among other factors, on who ultimately owns the asset and whether 
payment guarantees (such as minimum revenue guarantees or availability payments) are 
in place. 16 Second,  is the rationale of the PPP. Traditional PPPs are organised  around value 
for money, while innovation -oriented PPPs prioritise solving complex  problems and 
commercialising outcomes by pooling public and private capabilities. 17 
In the Commercial -Lite Approach, the government works with multiple prime contractors, 
which can be done under a traditional PPP. In the Commercial -Led Approach , the 
government focuses on supporting innovation through service -driven contracts or 
innovation -focused PPPs. In the Purely Commercial Approach, the contract management 
is centred  on the private enterprise as a provider of the service or product, more akin to a 
pure commercial transaction where the government buys the product or service as needed.  
In the Commercial -Lite Approach, private enterprise s openly compete to 
provide a solution that will be exclusive to the government, although 
some market dynamics are replicated  within the procurement process . In the Commercial -
Led Approach, companies first deliver a solution to the government that is then expected 
to serve non -government customers. The Purely Commercial Approach  would be 
epitomised by  the government procur ing a solution that is already available to non -

This programme characteristic focuses on the type of support for the

Governments also have long had program mes to fund research and early -stage capability

Regarding  Cost & Risk Structure , in a Commercial -Lite Approach, the 
government pays most of the development costs and takes on most of the 
risk, while industry contributes less and follows government direction. In a Commercial -
Led Approach , costs and risks are shared more evenly,  and the government helps de -risk 
specific phases by providing facilities, data, testing infrastructure, or milestone payments  
for capability development. In a Purely Commercial  model, industry funds the development 
of its solutions  and carries nearly all the risk, while the  government is one of many 
customers  with only typical buyer risk.

2.3 What are the public policy objectives behind a Commercial Procurement 
Model?  
Commercial procurement plays a crucial role in aligning government and industry 
capabilities with national interests. Commercial procurement is not only about procuring 
goods and services but also about ensuring that investments in space deliver long -term 
national benefits. The policy objectives behind such contracts set the guiding principles 
that influence how resources are allocated, risks are managed, and strategic outcomes are 
achieved.

Speed

It is important to note that these public policy objectives can be either explicit or implicit.  
Explicit/outspoken policy objectives  are those openly articulated in official documents 
such as national space policies, defence and industrial strategies, regulatory frameworks, 
and high -level public statements by government leaders . Implicit/indirect policy 
objectives  may not be formally stated but can be subjectively identified through 
transversal policy analysis  that looks across different sectors. These unde rlying objectives 
often reflect long -term strategic considerations, shaping geopolitical influence or subtly 
steering markets toward desired technological pathways. Taken together, these explicit 
and implicit objectives provide the underlying rationale for  a commercial -driven 
contracting approach in space. They highlight how governments seek to balance 
immediate operational needs with ambitions for autonomy, leadership and 
competitiveness in an increasingly contested and economically vital domain.  
A further question is whether such policy objectives are intended to foster existing 
capabilities  or to develop new capabilities  within a certain geographical or industrial 
context. ‘Existing capabilities’ often reinforce and scale up areas of established national 
strength, while  ‘new capabilities ’ may target emerging or less mature sectors where 
governments seek to stimulate novel industrial ecosystems.  
This distinction is important because it shapes how contracting mechanisms are designed 
— whether to consolidate market leaders, sustain industrial bases, or to seed and nurture 
new entrants and breakthrough technologies. For the scope of the paper, they a re defined 
as follows : 
Existing 
Capabilities  
Industrial, technological, and operational competencies are already 
established within a country or region when a commercial  procurement model 
is introduced. They rely on an industrial base with proven processes, supply 
chains, and skilled expertise. Policy objectives targeting existing capabilities 
aim to reinforce, scale, or sustain these strengths to preserve autonomy, ensure 
competitiveness, and secure continuity of suppl y in strategic sectors. This may

involve upgrading legacy systems, supporting market leaders, or preventing 
industrial decline.  
New 
capabilities  
Industrial, technological, or operational competencies that are not yet fully 
developed or embedded in the local ecosystem but are considered strategically 
important by policymakers. They may include emerging frontiers of the space 
economy, disruptive tech nologies, or areas where there has been prior 
dependency on third parties. Policy objectives targeting new capabilities are 
generally aimed at stimulating innovation, incentivising private investment, 
and lowering market -entry barriers for new actors.

2.4 What methodological framework will be used for the transversal analysis?  
Phase 2 of the study will perform a case study analysis across the example commercial 
procurement efforts and develop a cross -case comparison within and across both Europe 
and the  U.S. to highlight similarities or differences in approaches. Th is transversal, or cross -
case analysis, developed in Phase 2 will explore the interconnections between existing 
capabilities, the different approaches to commercial procurement, and the policy 
objectives driving them. It will capture how industrial capacity, strategic priorities, and 
commercial contracting approaches interact. Policy objectives will also be examined to 
determine whether they target existing capabilities, foster new ones, or both.  
As an early -stage  example, the European Copernicus Contributing Missions  (ECCM) largely 
follows the Purely Commercial Approach. Satellites are privately  (or at a minimum 
separately)  financed, developed, and operated by industry, and companies act as service 
providers to Copernicus. Purchases are made via ESA’s Dynamic Purchasing System using 
fixed-price call -offs for Earth -Observation data. The programme is not directly linked to 
government -funded capability development, giving industry strong autonomy to d esign 
systems and solutions and shoulder most of the costs and risks. That said, ECCM also 
shows elements of a Commercial -Led Approach. While ESA does not establish the 
requirements for the systems ex -ante, it is also not selecting what to buy directly from the 
market. The providers need to join the ECCM programme, and for that, they need to comply 
with certain standards. These lead to so -called “restricted competitions”. Therefore, it can 
still input some directionalit y into the ‘what’ when firms are desig ning their solutions. 
Overall, ECCM is best described as predominantly Purely Commercial, with minor 
deviations toward a Commercial -Led Approach . This provides a valuable example of how 
the spectrum  of methodology captures programme nuances.  
Among the United States  case studies,  an example of Commercial -Lite procurements for 
civil space purposes is the NASA xEMU , or spacesuit, program.  Spacesuit design and 
requirements differ depending on their purpose, most notably if they are to be worn in a

3 CONCLUSION  
The analysis presented in this paper demonstrates the breadth and complexity of how both 
the United States and Europe currently use the term “commercial” to apply to space 
activities. While both currently put significant emphasis, particularly in public st atements, 
about “going commercial”, in reality, that label is being applied to a wide range of 
programme structures and policy goals that at first glance seem somewhat disconnected 
from each other.  
To provide  clarity, we define Commercial Procurement as being distinct from traditional 
Bespoke Procurement , as characteri sed by the owner of the capability, the contract type, 
contract management model, customer(s), funding, and the government’s role in the 
development. Furthermore, we propose three different approaches within Commercial 
Procurement – Commercial -Lite, Commercial -Led, and Pure ly Commercial - that are 
defined by further variation of th eir characteristics. Finally, we identify multiple macro a nd 
micro public policy objectives that are established by governments (either explicitly or 
implicitly) to ensure that investments in space deliver long -term benefits and achieve 
strategic outcomes.  
This paper also identifies multiple examples of civil or defence  space programmes in both 
the United States and Europe that utili se one of the three Commercial Procurement 
approaches. For Phase 2 of this study, we plan to conduct a case study analysis to further 
examine these examples and how they map across public policy objectives in order to more 
fully understand how commercial pr ocurement models translate policy objectives into 
practical industrial and technological outcomes. In the process, we will ide ntify both 
similarities and differences between the United  States’  and European approaches and any 
recommended practices.  
In preparation for the second phase, we ask the community of stakeholders for 
feedback on our proposed definitions, conceptual framework, and selected case 
studies . We hope that this feedback will help us refine our work to ensure that it 
accurately reflects the most important aspects of this critical topic and can help bring 
more clarity to future discussions of how best to leverage commercial in the future.
