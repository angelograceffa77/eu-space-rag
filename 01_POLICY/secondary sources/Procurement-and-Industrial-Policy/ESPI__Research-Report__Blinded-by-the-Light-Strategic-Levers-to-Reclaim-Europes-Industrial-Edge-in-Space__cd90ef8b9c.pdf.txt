ABOUT
This report has been elaborated by ESPI as part of its mandate to provide independent analysis, 
proposals, and recommendations on strategic space issues to European decision makers and 
institutions. It expresses ESPI’s own views stemming from its research and analysis and informed 
by two high-level workshops held in Luxembourg and Berlin in mid-2025, with 33 high-level 
participants from industry, ESPI members, members of the ESPI Advisory Council and external 
experts, representing the full value chain of the European space industry and with a wide 
European footprint (see Page 64). Selected quotes from participants are featured throughout the 
document without attribution, and opinions expressed in this report do not represent individual 
views of any particular participant. Furthermore, the report considers related work of ESPI 
across its research agenda, such as its study on “The (Re)convergence of Europe’s space and 
defence industries”, “Space Venture 2024”, the Brief on “IRIS2 Growing Up: From Strategic Roots 
to Commercial Play”, and “An Initial Perspective on a European Space Strategy 2040: From Vision 
Towards Implementation”.

FOREWORD
Industrial capacity, financial markets, talent and skilled workforces provide the foundations for a competitive 
European space ecosystem required to innovate and to manufacture space infrastructures and to provide the 
services needed to fulfil the missions and purposes of space for Green and Sustainability, Security and Defence, 
and Exploration and Science. The digital economy, increasingly relying on space data and services, is becoming 
a critical component of national and European transformation. In this context, a growing European internal 
market, both institutional and commercial, is required to grow and maintain the European space industry and 
to avoid overdependence on foreign markets challenged by trade wars, the risks of takeover through foreign 
financing and acquisitions and the loss of Europe’s talent through brain drain.
It is over a year ago now that the report on the future of European competitiveness, prepared by Mario Draghi 
to guide future EU strategy, recognised space as a key strategic sector that will define Europe’s 
prosperity for decades to come.1 Political debate at the highest level acknowledged the critical 
situation of Europe’s space industry as a serious concern for the 
European economy. The ESPI Advisory Council recommended 
addressing this issue, underlining ESPI’s added value as an 
independent Think-Tank acting also beyond national and individual 
industrial interests.
This report has the objective to contribute to and widen the political 
debate on reclaiming Europe’s industrial edge in space. It suggests 
looking at the full value chain of space, from launchers and 
manufacturing to operations, data, and services, to address all industrial stakeholders, from mature actors to new 
entrants, from start-ups to large industries. It puts speed, agility and innovation at the core, as most critical for 
success in a global space race. It acknowledges the ability to scale up as the crucial next criterion for success, with 
governmental and B2B markets as a catalyst. It is clear that beyond B2B and B2G, the ability to conquer consumer 
mass markets is central to survival as part of the digital economy. It emphasises the challenge of protecting 
Europe’s knowledge assets and nurturing the next generation of talents.
The increasing role of security and defence in space is evident, attracting a lot of attention today, yet long-term 
competitiveness will depend on scaling beyond governmental markets. Emerging markets, such as direct-to-
device connectivity, and simultaneous prospects of industry consolidation, potentially driven by actors outside 
the space sector, like mobile network operators, point in this direction. At the same time, growth might not be 
limited to emerging mega-constellations, a multitude of small- to mid-size constellations are also expected and 
already in development, each tailored to specific mission needs (e.g. automotive) and demanding more 
flexible and disruptive procurement and business models. These procurement models go beyond the 
mere provision of hardware, addressing the full lifecycle of infrastructures and services and their 
evolution over time as turnkey solutions.
Taken together, these trends underline that reclaiming industrial edge cannot come from simply 
scaling up the existing offer and processes. It requires the agility and high dynamics of innovation 
of the entire sector to conquer markets, governmental and B2B2C. Competitiveness and sovereignty 
need each other, one cannot be sustainable without the other.
Europe still has all it takes to reclaim its edge in space. This report urges the space community to 
elaborate an (industrial) vision, reclaiming space as an enabler of prosperity and peace, and 
aims to provide an impulse for more substantiated and structured debate towards such 
a vision, between institutional and industrial actors, between the space community 
and policy makers.

HIGHLIGHTS
Europe in space is being blinded by the bright light of disruption of the global space ecosystem, 
brought by new industrial challengers like SpaceX, by rising space powers like China, and affecting 
even the most established institutional global actors like NASA and industries like Boeing.
For a long time, confidence in past success blinded Europe to the warning signs of global disruption 
and new technologies. Niche competitors and emerging disruptors were often and for too long 
dismissed as irrelevant until they redefined markets. The speed and intensity of disruption left 
leadership in government and industry without clear answers. A whole ecosystem of institutional 
actors and industry leaders, all used to decades of success in an environment dominated by 
government contracts, slow innovation cycles, and high-cost programmes, did not succeed in 
taking timely measures to adapt. Established industrial players often underestimated 
the speed and agility of actors of change. New entrants risked not translating 
ambitions and promises into sustainable business models. Governments focused 
preserving their heritage and lagged in adopting commercial 
partnerships at scale or reforming procurement models, until 
recent years, when external disruption started forcing a shift.

Europe’s Competitiveness and Innovation gaps are widening, 
and space is no exception. Once a global export leader across 
a spectrum of products and services, Europe is now facing a 
massive challenge in terms of receding market shares, low 
profitability and an ecosystem that is no longer able to keep 
pace with global disruptions and evolving needs. 
But, unlike in many other 
DeepTech industries, the balance of power is still ours to 
influence, as long as Europe acts with bravery and intent. 
In doing so, it should not only rely on increased defence 
expenditures for space as a cushion offering short-term relief, 
but as a springboard for new innovative products, solutions, 
and services with an ambition to reclaim global markets. 
Space today is exposed to two much larger sectors: defence and digital. Ultimately, the European 
space industry can only thrive if it can enable both commercial and public benefits. It needs, in 
particular, to demonstrate its value in propelling the creation and growth of European hyperscalers 
in a global digital economy, and show governments how it can help ensure Europe’s security and 
autonomy. 
The war in Ukraine underscores how indispensable space-based capabilities are to modern 
warfare, where it’s common practice for hundreds of space assets to be in sync, serving as a force 
multiplier in a single military operation. 
The space sector spreads technological innovations throughout the economy. When paired with 
other sectors, such as automotive, transportation, agrifood, pharmaceuticals, and digital, space 
supercharges innovation, boosts the wider economy well beyond the space sector, and creates 
under-accounted value to society.2  
Sovereignty and competitiveness are not bought off the shelf, 
however. They can only be attained, sustained and expanded based 
on an innovative, dynamic, and market-driven European industrial 
base guided by long-term clarity, a strict customer benefit focus, and 
a vision on capability and infrastructure needs.

This risk is exacerbated by the EU-US Trade Deal of July 2025, under which Europe commits to

To build a future free from crisis-driven reflexes, Europe must build an industrial policy that

third-country subsidiaries to deepen the continent’s dependencies.

For decades, Europe was considered a leading space power, 
demonstrating excellence and pioneering in both upstream and 
downstream segments. Despite relatively modest public budgets 
compared to other global players, Europe had built a competitive 
advantage by focusing on innovation and relying on a unique 
framework for collaboration between institutional and commercial 
space that made efficient use of public resources while leaning on 
commercial leadership. 
This approach enabled Europe to benefit from discounted strategic 
autonomy in critical areas, as it gave rise to a highly competitive space industry that has long 
dominated global commercial space markets.    
Today however, the European space industry is undergoing a profound transformation driven by 
endogenous and exogenous forces.
The European industry, in particular established players, faces mounting pressure from shifting market 
trends in commercial markets, disruption by “Neoprimes” establishing themselves as new market leaders,

Internally, the maturation and growth of the first European NewSpace wave has paved the way for

However, while the projected increase in defence expenditures across Europe may ease the

of the space sector. 
The moment should not be seen as a cushion, but as a catalyst to reimagine

1990s achieved growth and market dominance by 
successfully transforming the business model from 
specialised governmental and B2B connectivity

driven by significant innovation in SatCom

the cross-industry ecosystem collaboration, the

Video Broadcasting (DVB))  and the successful 
promotion of a Direct-to-Home (DTH) usage 
from GEO orbits. The resulting success and the 
move to privatise former intergovernmental 
(IGO) operators however had effects by the 
2010s, shifting focus from transformation to 
consolidation, from innovation push to a focus 
on shareholder value, harvesting mass market 
success to maximise short-term commercial profit. 
The long-term contract and revenue backlog (on 
average 10-15 years) created by the successful 
broadcast business attracted a different set of 
shareholders with low risk tolerance (e.g., pension 
funds) looking for long-term stable returns and 
favourable financial ratios.

were good, and all should stay as is…”.

This brings Europe’s space industry to an inflection 
point, driven by mounting challenges, but also by new 
opportunities, with the defence and digital sectors showing a 
new level of interest in space, providing a unique window of 
opportunity to the full space industrial complex to innovate and 
scale. 
As space returns to the 
forefront of global geopolitical 
and geoeconomic ambitions, massive investments in the United 
States and China are already enabling their space industries to 
benefit from large-scale programmes, based on which they can 
expand commercially, undermining the market positions of the 
European industry. 
For now, Europe’s response is slow, stepwise, and siloed; treating 
the launcher crisis, the economic viability of its manufacturing 
base, and the need for unrestricted access to secure satcom one 
by one, but not as part of an overall policy and industrial strategy.  
Europe’s past discount on strategic autonomy now comes at a cost. At a time of geopolitical shifts 
and trade wars, Europe’s underinvestment in security, defence, and resilience now represents 
a major handicap, as global public investment in space is overtaking civilian space budgets; 
Europe’s gap in strategic capabilities is widening fast.  
Compounding this handicap is Europe’s fixation on normative behaviour, rule-setting, and 
governance, without building the capabilities that give those efforts force and legitimacy. 
Europe is faced with a perfect storm. This is the moment to prove its 
strengths, re-focus its vision, and act.
The continent has all it takes to reposition itself as a force of innovation, 
develop best-in-class sovereign capabilities, as proven with Galileo, 
and nurture a thriving commercial base fit to win global markets in a 
digital economy. 
Even today, Europe remains the supplier of choice for high-end 
space systems such as state-of-the-art Earth observation systems, 
pressurised space station modules, ground station services, as well as 
high-performance enabling components such as laser communication 
terminals, dispenser systems, or electric propulsion.

While institutional markets remain by far the largest source of revenue for the space industry, 
industrial competitiveness is the only key to sustainable and long-lasting sovereignty. The pace 
of innovation in competitive environments outpaces that of traditional institutional procurement 
programmes with rigidly defined specifications and limited incentive 
to improve performance and cost once the contract is sealed.
Sovereignty itself is also critical to enabling further industry growth 
and expansion. Public ambitions, backed by clear needs and 
commitments, including anchor customer schemes, form the backbone 
on which companies can build to scale up and commercially expand. 
In Europe, sovereignty and competitiveness are too often wrongly 
presented as separate pursuits, for example, questioning “what is this 
launcher for? Is it to make life easy for commercial satellite operators 
or is it to assure European launcher autonomy” in the development 
of Ariane 6.6  It is essential that public programmes not only ensure 
autonomy, but simultaneously create opportunities for competition, 
ensure innovation and keep Europe’s technological edge in an 
increasingly competitive global sector. 
When discussing future pathways for Europe’s space sector, many call 
for a dedicated industrial policy for space. A tailored approach is 
needed as space is a unique sector at the 
intersection of public purpose and private 
gain. Moreover, space services are increasingly integrated into broader 
domains such as energy, transport, digital, agriculture, next to defence 
and climate, no different to AI and energy storage technologies. Their 
trajectory will increasingly define the competitiveness and strategic 
posture of Europe as a whole.
For such an industrial policy to be effective, it must address the 
full value chain, from upstream manufacturing to downstream 
applications, including the end-user segment. This framing, now 
echoed in the European Commission’s Vision for the European Space 
Economy, is essential not only to preserve industrial capabilities but 
also to expand demand, stimulate innovation, and ensure long-term 
strategic relevance.
When charting a path forward, Europe must reflect on the foundations 
of its past success. The origins of Europe’s space programmes, and the positive trajectory following 
the Symphonie case, offer important lessons, particularly the lucid recognition that autonomy and 
commercial opportunities reinforce one another and are key to an innovative and competitive space 
industry. Reclaiming this relationship will be key.

As early as 1960, European leaders, recognised the 
need for European industry to “participate in the 
commercial developments resulting from space 
technology” (CoE, Recommendation 251). In 1961, 
Lord Thorneycroft highlighted that the creation of 
ELDO was crucial to “avoid a situation in which 
Europe is right out of the launching of any satellite 
in the future,” describing it as “an opportunity, 
which otherwise would have been denied to us, of 
taking some part in the commercial exploitation of 
space”. 
The well-known Symphonie case in the 1970s 
illustrated this in practice. First initiated through 
a political commitment between President de

Comparing two major grant instruments in Europe and the United States, the European Innovation 
Council and the Small Business Innovation Research programme (SBIR), highlights the issue. Both use 
two key performance indicators related to speed: (i) time to inform, that is the period between the 
closure of a call for proposals and the date when applicants are told the results, and (ii) time to grant, 
meaning the period between the closure of the call and the signing of the grant agreement.
The first difference is that EIC targets are less ambitious. As a baseline, U.S. institutions aim to inform 
companies sooner, which can be decisive for whether to pursue a product or service or pivot. When it 
comes to the time it takes to sign a grant agreement, SBIR again sets a more ambitious and consistent 
target. In 2024, the EIC Accelerator shortened its target from 8 to 6 months, but the EIC Pathfinder still 
retains the 8-month objective. The second difference is that performance in the European programme 
has not met the defined KPIs: SBIR has generally met its targets, while within the EIC only the Pathfinder 
has consistently done so, and even then, against less ambitious timelines.
