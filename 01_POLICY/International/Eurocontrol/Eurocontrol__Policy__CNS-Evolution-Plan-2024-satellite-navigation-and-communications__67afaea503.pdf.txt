Executive summary 
The CNS Evolution Plan will become the strategic reference to guide the modernisation 
and optimisation of the European Communications, Navigation and Surveillance (CNS) 
infrastructure, translating the vision established by the European ATM Master Plan 
2025 Edition into an actionable roadmap. 
This first version, Edition 2024, addresses Initiating Action 2 of the CNS Advisory 
Group Action Plan by defining the structure and content of the Evolution Plan, laying 
the groundwork for a more comprehensive strategy and outlining a structured 
methodology for its further refinement. 
Future editions will build on this foundation to ensure that CNS systems evolve in a 
coordinated, efficient and forward -looking manner to meet the operational and 
strategic objectives of European and global airspace management. 
In addition to defining a structure and methodology for the development of the 
Evolution Plan, the CNS Programme Manager has already developed three aspects of 
the current edition of the Evolution Plan and tested the first steps of the proposed 
methodology. 
Firstly, a review of the vision and strategic drivers for CNS evolution was conducted, 
which highlighted the need to develop CNS systems that support the vision of the 
European ATM Master Plan, ICAO's Global Air Navigation Plan (GANP) and EASA's 
European Plan for Aviation Safety (EPAS) and ensure compliance with European and 
global regulatory frameworks. 
In particular, it will be necessary  to develop a complementary and detailed planning 
view to the high -level CNS roadmap in support of  the ATM Master Plan Strategic 
Deployment Objectives #7 (Transition towards high performance of air -ground 
connectivity) and #9 (CNS optimisation, modernisation and resilience). 
Secondly, the CNS Programme Manager initiated a survey of its stakeholders to verify 
the existence of issues with the current CNS infrastructure and its evolution, and the 
need to address them through the development of the CNS Evolution Plan to achieve 
the aforementioned vision and strategic objectives. This issue draws the attention of 
the stakeholder community to short term issues identified by the CNS Programme 
Manager as part of his work.

1 Introduction 1.1 CNS Advisory Group Report (1) The CNS Advisory Group was established by DG MOVE and started to work in 2020 to develop recommendations for a better organisation and management of the CNS infrastructure, noting the well-documented issues associated with the implementation of past EU CNS mandates.  (2) The CNS Advisory Group Report [1] was reviewed by Operational stakeholders, and representatives of the manufacturing industry and other stakeholders. (3) Key issues Identified by the CNS Advisory Group: a) Pan-European CNS deployment is slow. b) The CNS infrastructure redundancy layers and geographic locations are not optimised. c) The variety of airspace users’ needs is an important consideration for the efficient deployment. d) CNS infrastructure is not sufficiently spectrum-efficient and is not sufficiently protected against physical and cyber security threats. e) CNS interoperability requirements and compliance demonstration have not been fully addressed. The lack of a harmonised compliance framework resulted in additional delays, duplication of efforts, and costs for airspace users and ANSPs. f) There is a lack of European wide CNS Programme Management addressing the full life cycle of the CNS infrastructure. (4) Report findings: a) The CNS advisory group is fully aware of the fact that the CNS infrastructure is not delivering operational benefits directly; it is a technical enabler to provide operational benefits to ATM and airspace users. The evolution of the CNS infrastructure, including both deployment of new CNS systems and optimisation of some legacy CNS systems, is needed to achieve the long-term objectives of ATM modernisation in support of the evolution of Air Traffic as defined in the European ATM Master Plan. b) The current CNS infrastructure provides a robust and resilient service. However, occasionally CNS outages can cause a significant degradation of ATM services. c) The targeted CNS infrastructure will be composed of an optimised combination of ground and space-based technologies, complemented with Minimal Operational Networks (MONs) of conventional infrastructure that are needed to maintain services in case outages occur in the primary infrastructure (e.g. interferences affecting satellite navigation signals). (5) Overall, the Advisory Group made a series of recommendations, requiring buy-in from the complete stakeholder community if they were to be met. One of these recommendations was to ‘’ Translate the Master Plan’s CNS roadmap into a ‘CNS evolution plan’ with short, medium and long-term objectives, priorities and decision points.’’ These recommendations were accompanied by an 18-point “Action Plan for the development and management of the implementation of the European

CNS Evolution Plan is fully consistent with the European ATM Master Plan that provides the vision for ATM evolution in Europe. The evolution and operation of the CNS infrastructure should also directly contribute to the decarbonisation of aviation in line with the EU Green Deal objectives and facilitate the integration of new entrants and technologies. The solutions and technologies selected in the CNS Evolution Plan should be suitable to support operational improvements in all anticipated operational scenarios  (2) This initial version of the CNS Evolution Plan is a preliminary version and serves as a foundational document outlining the key objectives of CNS evolution in Section 3. It highlights the current challenges and sets the groundwork for the future, more comprehensive CNS Evolution Plan development. (3) The CNS Evolution Plan is consistent with key European and global strategic documents such as the European ATM Master Plan, ICAO’s Global Air Navigation Plan (GANP), EASA’s European Plan for Aviation Safety (EPAS), the Network Strategy Plan, and the European Radio Navigation Plan. These plans serve as the strategic drivers outlined in Section 2 of this document. The complete CNS Evolution Plan will not only align with the aforementioned strategic frameworks but will also actively contribute to their evolution by identifying necessary changes and proposing improvements., (4) In line with the overall planning outlined in the CNS Advisory Group Action Plan (Figure 4) [3], this edition of the CNS Evolution Plan relates to initiation action 2, in particular the important aspects of definition of the structure and content of the CNS Evolution Plan, including a methodology for its development and maintenance included in Appendix 1.

This horizon reflects the evolving nature of aviation technology, airspace management needs, and the increasing demand for sustainability and operational efficiency. 2 Vision and Strategic drivers of CNS Evolution (1) The development of the CNS Evolution Plan will address the operational needs and requirements of ATM in Europe and globally, ensuring the modernisation of CNS infrastructure in alignment with the evolving demands of airspace operations. This modernisation is critical for achieving the long-term goals of environmental sustainability, operational efficiency, and seamless coordination across all domains of air traffic management.  (2) The CNS Evolution Plan will support the development of a modern, interoperable, and efficient digital European sky, driven by the vision set forth in the European ATM Master Plan and aligned with ICAO’s Global Air Navigation Plan (GANP), European Plan for Aviation Safety (EPAS), Network Strategy Plan, and European Radio Navigation Plan. (3) CNS Evolution Plan tends to optimise CNS infrastructure in Europe and, where reasonable, to optimise infrastructure where it does not negatively affect aviation safety, ATM capacity nor national security, taking into account global security situation which is relevant to EU countries. (4) As the first edition of the CNS Evolution Plan, it aims to establish a foundation for alignment with the broader goals of global and regional air traffic management systems. The full edition of CNS Evolution Plan (2026) will be designed to be responsive to the evolving needs of the aviation industry, addressing key challenges. 2.1 European ATM Master Plan (1) The CNS Evolution Plan is fully aligned with the ATM Master Plan’s vision [4] of making Europe the most efficient and environmentally friendly sky to fly in the world by 2045. By addressing the detailed requirements for CNS infrastructure, it will enhance the roadmap's ability to meet the dynamic needs of emerging applications, innovative air mobility (IAM), and increasingly complex airspace operations. In this context, the CNS Evolution Plan will act as a bridge, ensuring the operationalisation of the high-level strategies within the ATM Master Plan and facilitating the coordinated deployment of CNS solutions across Europe. (2) The CNS Evolution Plan will serve as a complementary and detailed planning view to the high-level CNS roadmap outlined in the European ATM Master Plan 2025 Edition. The Master Plan, particularly through its focus on Strategic Deployment Objectives (SDO) shown on Figure 5, such as SDO7 on transition towards high performance of air-ground connectivity (multilink) and SDO9 on CNS optimisation, modernisation, and resilience, already establishes the strategic direction for CNS improvements.  (3) SDO 7 – Transition towards high performance of air-ground connectivity (multilink) (4) This strategic deployment objective aims to improve air-ground datalink performance, accelerate technology adoption, and achieve economies of scale while reducing the fragmentation that characterises current datalink implementation. (5) SDO 9 - CNS optimisation, modernisation and resilience

More detailed information about consistency between CNS Evolution Plan 2024 and the European ATM Master Plan 2025 is presented in Appendix 3. 2.2 ICAO Global Air Navigation Plan (1) The ICAO Global Air Navigation Plan (GANP) serves as a critical framework for defining key Air Traffic Management (ATM) operational requirements, driving global harmonisation and modernisation of airspace operations. The GANP, particularly through the Aviation System Block Upgrades (ASBU) framework (Figure 6), provides a strategic roadmap for the phased deployment of CNS technologies. (2) The following CNS elements are represented in the current 6th Edition of the GANP [5]: (3) Surveillance Systems (ASUR) with the aim of modernising and enhancing surveillance capabilities, particularly in remote and oceanic regions. a) Current (Block 0): Use of ADS-B OUT and Multilateration (MLAT) systems, complementing traditional radar. b) Future (Blocks 1-3): ADS-B surveillance via space-based systems and the evolution of vehicle-to-vehicle surveillance for autonomous operations. By Block 3, performance-based surveillance will allow for flexible use of surveillance technologies tailored to specific airspace needs. 

EUROCONTROL  Network Management Directorate

(4) Communications Infrastructure (COMI) focused on improving and expanding communications infrastructure, integrating new technologies to support higher capacity and efficiency. a) Current (Block 0): Use of VHF, HF, and SATCOM for voice communications, with increasing reliance on data links like VDL Mode 2 and SATCOM Class C for messaging. b) Future (Blocks 1-3): Introduction of multi-frequency VDL Mode 2 and ATN/IPS for data exchange, eventually leading to broadband communications and SATCOM Class A systems for robust, global coverage and increased capacity. (5) ATS Communications Service (COMS) aiming to move from voice-based to data link communications, improving air-ground communications and reducing congestion. a) Current (Block 0): Introduction of Controller-Pilot Data Link Communications (CPDLC) for reducing voice channel congestion, particularly in procedural airspace. b) Future (Blocks 1-3): Full extension of CPDLC and ADS-C systems, leading to increased automation of aircraft systems and trajectory synchronisation, supporting dynamic and flexible airspace operations. (6) Navigation Systems (NAVS) designed to advance satellite-based and ground-based navigation systems, ensuring precision and resilience in all phases of flight. a) Current (Block 0): Use of GNSS with augmentations like SBAS and GBAS, and introduction of Minimal Operating Networks for optimisation of conventional navigation aid infrastructure. b) Future (Blocks 1-4): Implementation of Dual-Frequency Multi-Constellation (DF/MC) GNSS for greater robustness. Alternative PNT (A-PNT) solutions will be developed to act as complementary means to GNSS during disruptions, ensuring navigation continuity.

(2) A key aspect of the CNS Evolution Plan is ensuring that the development and implementation of new CNS technologies contribute to the safety goals outlined in EPAS. These goals include fostering systemic safety and resilience, managing risk interdependencies, and improving safety management. The CNS Evolution

Strengthening CNS system resilience by implementing robust cybersecurity protocols and backup systems, with the goal of mitigating cyber threats and ensuring secure airspace and safe flight operations.  
Why? 
As the aviation industry continues to evolve through the advancement of CNS systems, the challenges related to safety, security and defence are becoming more complex. Promote gradual replacement of

Why? 
Technological obsolescence is a growing concern for the aviation 
industry, as it seeks to modernise its CNS systems while maintaining 
operational safety and efficiency. Current CNS systems, while 
reliable and proven, are increasingly unable to support the continued 
growth in airspace and airport usage expected over the coming 
decades. 
As the aviation industry transitions to modern CNS systems, there is 
an inherent risk of disruption or failure during the adoption process. 
New technologies, while rigorously tested and approved before 
deployment, introduce unfamiliar protocols, systems integration 
challenges, and greater alignment with technologies used in other 
sectors. This alignment increases the risk of vulnerabilities being 
shared across industries. Therefore, a phased transition is necessary 
to avoid a sudden shift to new systems that could cause widespread 
failures. A mix of legacy and new systems will be required to prevent 
single points of failure in the case of unexpected disruptions to 
newer technologies. 
Given the high costs of new equipment, many operators may 
continue to rely on older, non-state-of-the-art technologies for as 
long as possible. This creates a fragmented operating environment 
where some aircraft are equipped with the latest CNS capabilities 
while others continue to use outdated systems, complicating efforts 
to achieve seamless and efficient air traffic management.

Why? 
The radio frequency spectrum is a scarce natural resource with finite 
capacity limits and for which demand is constantly increasing. 
In the context, the introduction of 5G technology has already 
highlighted potential conflicts between non-aeronautical radio 
spectrum needs and aeronautical stakeholders, but also the potential 
harmful interference with critical onboard equipment such as radio 
altimeters. 
Furthermore, the lack of efficient use of the spectrum dedicated to 
aviation safety and lack of adaptability of avionics to the evolving 
spectrum environment will expose aviation CNS services further to 
interference and, inevitably, to increase in costs to deploy fixes. 
Further growth in commercial aviation and the increased data 
demands makes it important to improve and optimise avionics and 
CNS equipment, incorporating spectrum efficient and compliant RF 
(Radio Frequency) technologies. This optimisation/modernisation 
effort will make it possible for CNS systems to evolve to meet the 
growth of civil air transport as well as to accommodate the 
introduction of new aviation uses in a more environmentally 
sustainable manner.

assignment of specific frequencies within the allocated spectrum. For 
aviation, ensuring interference-free access to relevant frequency 
bands is crucial to maintaining the reliability of CNS systems and air-
ground interactions. Interference from non-aviation sources, such as 
mobile telecommunications, can significantly disrupt operations and 
compromise safety.

1.3  CNS Evolution Plan Development Steps 
 Definition of the vision 
(1) It is proposed to develop a vision in the introductory chapter of the CNS Evolution
Plan.
(2) The vision paints a picture of the future. It answers the question, “Where do we
want to go?” A well-crafted vision describes the desired state or outcom
e.
(3) It is proposed to dedicate one of the first CNS Evolution Plan working sessions
to develop such a vision for the CNS infrastructure (at 2030, 2040 and 2050 time
horizons).
(4) The CNS Advisory Group report [1] and  the European ATM Master Plan [4]
provide elements that could be reused in this exercise; however, the resultin g
vision should remain concise and impactful. Ideally, it should be a few sentences
or a short paragraph.
(5) The CNS Evolution Plan will outline a strategy how to achieve this vision. It will
answer the question, “How will we get there?”.
 Definition of the problems 
(1) The first step is to verify the existence of problems with the current CNS
infrastructure and its evolution, and a need to address them through the
development of the CNS Evolution Plan to achieve that above vision, i.e. the
desired end state. The problem analysis is a crucial step as only a correct
diagnosis of the problem and its causes can lead us to the appropriate response.
If the problem is ill -defined, it is unlikely that the next stages would identify
relevant objectives or effective options.
(2) The objective of the problem definition phase is to answer the followin g
questions:
(3) What are the issues or problems which we are trying to solve through the CNS
Evolution Plan?
(4) What is the scale of the problems? Are they relevant or not? Are the problems
local or global?
(5) What are the root causes underlying the issues or problems?
(6) Who is concerned by the problems, how and to what extent?
(7) How may the problems develop if nothing is done in addition to the acti ons
already taken or planned by operational stakeholders, states or other parti es
involved?
(8) The CNS Advisory Group report (in particular its recommendations #3 to #7) and
the CNS PM MON Concept and Design Criteria document [10] provide elements
that could be reused to feed the problem analysis and develop the problem
statement; however, it is important to avoid “backward engineering” where t he
problem analysis is performed with a specific outcome in mind.
(9) First, a survey has been initiated with contributors to collect arguments and
problem perceptions that will need to be further analysed. Problems should far
as possible be supported by tangible evidence (ideally information collect ed
based on verifiable an d reliable methods by trustworthy and neutral sources).

Stakeholder views are often reflecting interests of certain groups of 
stakeholders but can complement objective data by giving an indication of the 
relative importance of the problems. 
(10) It is then proposed to dedicate at least one CNS Evolution Plan working session
to the problem analysis.
(11) In the case of CNS, it is expected that problems and drivers will be numerous ,
complex or interrelated. Wherever possible, the problem analysis should try t o
disentangle complex problems into several simpler ones that perhaps can be
addressed separately an d use visual aids (such as problem trees) to describe
them and to link them through to the (later to be defined) objectives and options.
(12) After completing the problem analysis, and prior to set objectives and consider
options, the exercise should be summarised through a succinct problem
statement that will contextualise the problems and explain why they matter.
 Definition of the objectives 
(1) Objectives link the problems and their drivers to the options. Setting objectives
helps to set the level of ambition and fix the yardsticks for comparing options.
(2) Objectives can be general (e.g. “Improve the cost efficiency of CNS systems”),
or specific and setting concretely what the CNS Evolution Plan is meant t o
achieve. For each problem identified above, there should be at least one, but
most likely a set of specific objectives.
(3) When setting objectives, notably specific objectives, it can be useful to reflect on
the S.M.A.R.T criteria: objectives should be Specific, Measurable, Achievable
,
Relevant and Time-bound (i.e. ‘S.M.A.R.T’). Still, they should be broad enough to
allow consideration of all relevant alternatives without prejudging a particular
solution.
 Identification of options 
(1) Identifying alternative options will be an iterative process. The aim is to consider
as many realistic alternatives as possible and then narrow them down to t he
most relevant ones for further analysis.
(2) The following four steps are suggested to identify a realistic set of options:
a) Construct a baseline from which the impacts of the options will be
assessed.
b) Start by compiling a wide range of alternative options.
c) Identify the most viable options; explain the discarded options.
d) Describe in reasonable detail the key aspects of the retained options t o
allow an in-depth analysis of the associated impacts.

than a “do nothing” scenario, this would more correctly be described as a

2.1 ICAO Standards Framework and Global Coordination  
(1) In the context of Communications, Navigation, and Surveillance (CNS) systems,
ICAO plays a pivotal role in establishing global standards and practices essential
for maintaining safety, efficiency, and interoperability in international aviati on.
The effective functioning of global aviatio n relies heavily on the robust
framework of standards and recommended practices (SARPs) established b y
ICAO. As the aviation industry faces increasing demands for capacity and
integration of new technologies, ICAO's commitment to developing ,
coordinating, and maintaining these standards is vital for the seaml ess
integration and operation of CNS systems across all regions. This commitment
is further emphasised by ICAO Assembly Resolution A41 -8, which underscores
the importance of enhancing CNS resilience by maximising the integration of all
suitable ground, space, and airborne components in a complementary and
cooperative manner. This includes improving technological capabilities, civil -
military operational procedures, and coordination with radi o regulat ory
authorities to ensure the safe and efficient operation of CNS systems.
(2) SARPs related to CNS are developed through a collaborative process that
involves member states, industry stakeholders, and technical experts. These
standards are embedded within several key Annexes to the Chicago Convention,
most notably Annex 10 (Aeronautical Telecommunications), which is dedicated
to CNS systems. Annex 10 is divided into volumes addressing different aspects
of CNS, including Radio Navigation Aids, communications procedures,
communications systems, Surveillance and Collision Avoidance Syst ems, and
Spectrum utilisation (Figure 21). For the CNS Evolution Plan, it is crucial t
o
consider the ongoing revisions and updates to these SARPs, which are driven by
the need to accommodate new technologies. These evolving standards ensure
that CNS systems remain interoperable, resilient, and capable of suppo rting
future air traffic demands.
(3) To ensure that SARPs related to CNS systems are effectively implemented, ICAO
employs several monitoring and auditing mechanisms. The Universal Safet y
Oversight Audit Programme (USOAP) plays a key role in assessing how well
member states adhere to CNS -related standards. The audit findings provide
insights into areas where further support or capacity -building might be needed,
particularly in regions with limited infrastructure or technical expertise.
Additionally, ICAO's regional air navigation plans provide  detailed guidance for
the implementation of CNS systems at the regional level. These plans are aligned
with the ICAO Global Air Navigation Plan (GANP) and are regularly updated t
o
reflect changes in technology and operational requirements. The coordination of
these plans ensures that CNS systems across different regions are interoperable
and capable of supporting global aviation operations.
(4) As CNS technologies advance, ICAO is adapting its standards-making processes
to accommodate new capabilities while ensuring interoperability and safety .
This involves the development of performance -based standards and the
adoption of industry standards whe re applicable. The ICNSS -TF is currently
working on streamlining the standardi sation framework which is crucial for

integration by ensuring that these systems are capable of withstanding 
disruptions and continue to provide reliable service across all phases of flight. 
As the aviation industry l ooks toward the future, ICAO is actively engaged in 
addressing the challenges posed by emerging CNS technologies. These 
developments require continuous updates to SARPs to accommodate new 
capabilities while ensuring backward compatibility with existing systems.

2.4 ITU Radio Regulations Framework 
(1) The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) carries out the global
management of radio frequency spectrum, which is vital for the functioning of
Communications, Navigation, and Surveillance (CNS) systems in aviation. The
regulatory framework established by the ITU, particularly through the Radi o
Regulations (RR), provides the legal foundation for the allocation and use of
radio frequencies worldwide, which in tu rn is transposed to national legislati on
and enforced in each contracting Member State. The n ational administration
responsible for this is typically the telecommunications regulator.

EUROCONTROL Network Management Directorate 
Page: 57 
(2) The RR enacts provisions to protect aeronautical frequencies from interference
that could jeopardise safety and disrupt operations by instituting a number of
technical and administrative conditions on the assignment and coordination of
frequencies, which a re again the responsibility of the telecommunicati ons
regulator to enforce. Furthermore, the RRs also implement specific measures to
ensure that the use of aeronautical frequencies is compliant with the aforesaid
technical and administrative measures by re quiring CNS equipment to b
e
compliant with the ICAO SARPs. The telecommunications and aviati on
frameworks are therefore interconnected to assure that aviation’s uni que
spectrum needs are met globally while maintaining avoiding interference wit h
other spectrum users.
(3) The collaboration between ITU and ICAO is thus essential to safeguard the
integrity of the radio frequencies that are critical for CNS systems, and to ensure
ongoing access to radio spectrum to meet future Air Traffic Management and
other aviation needs.
(4) The ITU’s World Radiocommunication Conferences (WRCs) are the ITU’s
instrument for maintaining the RRs. These conferences, held nominally every
four years, review and revise the Radio Regulations, and they address spectrum
allocation requests from the vari ous sectors including broadcasting, sci ence
services, International Mobile Telephony (e.g. 5G), licence free services (e.g. Wi-
Fi), government services (such as police, border forces, emergency e tc),
maritime and aviation services, among others. ICAO activ ely participates i n
WRCs as an observing party to represent the radio spectrum interests of aviation,
advocating for the protection of aviation spectrum allocations and following
decisions that affect CNS systems. The outcomes of these conferences directly
influence the availability and integrity of the radio spectrum for the continued
safe operation of CNS systems worldwide.
(5) As the representative delegations in the WRC are the telecommunicati ons
administrations of each Member State, careful and timely coordination between
telecoms and aviation stakeholders is essential. ICAO facilitates this
coordination by preparing a (global ) Common Aviation Position for each WRC
(Figure 24), through its expert body, the Frequency and Spectrum Managemen
t
Panel which is introduced above. This makes the global and regional
coordination between ITU and Aviation Community a cornerstone of global
aviation safety.
