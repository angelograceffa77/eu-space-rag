LETTER FROM THE PRESIDENT OF THE GOVERNMENT
The nature of conflicts has evolved. We are witnessing this transformation in 
Ukraine and the Middle East, as well as in many other regions across the globe. 
Consequently, we must be prepared to confront the new threats that lie ahead.
One of the areas where these changes are most evident is aerospace. Here, 
emerging geopolitical dynamics, the rapid adoption of advanced technologies, and 
pressing environmental challenges define a new context—one that compels us to 
look toward the future with ambition and fresh perspectives.
By its very nature, the aerospace sector is dual-use, serving both civil and military 
purposes. In our daily lives, numerous technologies originally developed for military 
applications—such as radar, navigation systems, and satellite communications—
are indispensable. The reverse is also true: civil and military aeronautical and space 
industries are deeply interconnected. For this reason, security cannot be separated 
from industrial policy.
Security in the aerospace domain is equally critical for sectors vital to Spain’s 
economy, such as tourism. This concept of security must be understood broadly: 
encompassing deterrence and air defense capabilities, health protection, cyber and 
supply chain security, and responses to threats such as terrorism and organized 
crime. Furthermore, training and attracting talent in aerospace disciplines is an 
essential component of our security framework.
Pedro Sánchez
President of the Government of Spain
Spain must position itself as a nation committed to air and space, embracing 
aerospace development from a security perspective. In today’s context of 
uncertainty and intensifying geostrategic competition, the 2025 National Aerospace 
Security Strategy provides high-level guidance to foster collaboration among all 
key stakeholders—ranging from public administrations and the aerospace sector 
to our international allies.

Spain and Europe as a whole are intensely affected by increasing global geopolitical 
instability. The international order is undergoing changes of a magnitude not seen 
since 1945, and we cannot remain passive onlookers before the new global order 
that is rapidly taking shape. Instead, we must actively participate in constructing 
the emerging international structure. But to play a decisive role in shaping the new 
international order to reflect our own values and interests amid growing competition 
among powers of different stripes, we must strengthen our capabilities and reduce 
strategic dependencies in order to mitigate significant vulnerabilities that currently 
affect our security.
Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine has starkly exposed our shortcomings 
and vulnerabilities. Only through joint efforts to strengthen our capabilities will we 
reduce the severe dependencies that are now preventing us from making independent 
decisions and which, ultimately, could jeopardise our future.

The aerospace sector is one of the areas that most clearly reveals these vulnerabilities 
and dependencies. The White Paper on European Defence, presented by the European 
Commission on 19 March 2025, at the request of the European Council, articulates a 
compact and coherent strategy that includes joint, ambitious and necessary measures 
to address these deficiencies over the next five years. This is a true emergency plan, 
one that will mitigate decades-long shortcomings if its proposals and approaches are 
implemented by us all, working together.
The 2025 National Aerospace Security Strategy (NASS) is the second national strategy 
in this area, succeeding the first of its kind, approved in 2019. Since then, rapid and 
significant changes in the air and space domains have had a direct impact on the 
aerospace sector, which is of vital importance to national security. This dynamic of 
change underscores the need to review the 2019 NASS, as well as the aerospace-related 
provisions included in the 2021 National Security Strategy (NSS), with a view to 
expanding existing provisions and to incorporating new aspects related to aerospace 
security, within and beyond the atmosphere.
In this regard, the 2021 NSS proposed a line of action to ensure the security of global 
common spaces, via the creation of a Spanish Space Agency. This line of action was 
formally defined by Royal Decree 158/2023, which approved the Statute for the Spanish 
Space Agency, with a component dedicated to national security. Moreover, Line of 
Action No. 3 of the 2021 NSS recommended “ Developing the industrial complex for 
defence, security and space, and developing dual technologies...” The importance of this 
focus is highlighted by the significant recent shift toward industrial policies aimed at 
ensuring greater economic security at the international level. Therefore, it is necessary 
to define new strategic lines of action in the field of aerospace security.
The Industrial and Technological Plan for Security and Defence, approved by the 
Council of Ministers in April 2025, is Spain’s response to the current international 
context, within the Government’s firm commitment to the security of its citizens, to 
the country’s technological progress and to furthering the European project.

Like its 2019 predecessor, the 2025 NASS considers it advisable to view aerospace 
security as a single question, both to effectively address threats and to mitigate 
common and interdependent risks within and beyond the terrestrial airspace.
However, in a context of increasing competition at all levels, this integrated approach 
requires us to recognise, simultaneously, that outer space represents a distinct 
domain, where threats to services essential to national security have intensified. Both 
the European Union and NATO have denominated outer space as a distinct operational 
domain, while implementing a comprehensive and integrative approach to terrestrial 
airspace and outer space to ensure the effective coordination and management of 
resources.
The Strategy is divided into four chapters. The first, entitled “ Contextual analysis of 
the air and space domains”, presents general aspects, briefly describes the regulatory 
framework and sets out the need for a single aerospace security strategy to protect 
key national interests in both these domains.
The second chapter, entitled “Threats to National Security in the Domains  of Air and 
Space,” addresses the concepts of risk and threat in the field of aerospace. It identifies 
the main vulnerabilities which arise from the nature of these domains: their level of 
interconnectivity, the highly technological nature of the operational infrastructure, 
the dependence on industrial capabilities, and the reality of constant technological 
advances.
Due to the unique characteristics of the aerospace sector, many of the threats that may 
arise lie outside Spanish sovereignty and jurisdiction, making it necessary at all times to 
consider both national responsibilities and Spain’s international commitments.
The third chapter, “Objectives,” details the main strategic targets for the 2021 NSS, 
setting out the following objectives and lines of action for the aerospace sector. 
• Strategic Objective 1:  Protect Spain in and from air and space, with the 
fundamental understanding that Spain should “PROTECT people’s lives, 
rights and freedoms, and defend the Constitutional order”.

• Strategic Objective 2: Improve security and defence capabilities in the air 
and in space, with the understanding that Spain should “PROMOTE the

INTRODUCTION 
In 2019, the National Security Council (CSN) approved the first National Aerospace 
Security Strategy (NASS). Among its main innovations was the proposal to create the 
National Aerospace Security Council (CNSA), modelled after similar bodies established 
in other areas of national security. Supporting the CSN, the CNSA evaluates the 
progress and implementation of the NASS.
In the course of its work, the CNSA identified the need to update the NASS. This 
proposal was approved by the CSN, for the following reasons.
First, the global strategic context is undergoing significant geopolitical transformations, 
characterised by a notable increase in regional tensions and conflicts. In this respect, 
the current situation highlights the great difficulty in ensuring the security of terrestrial 
airspace and outer space, given the emergence of new threats to space capabilities 
and the proliferation of vectors that, although mentioned in the 2019 NASS, have since 
undergone significant expansion and technological evolution.

16
In the case of drones, there has been exponential growth, both quantitatively and 
qualitatively, in their use in military and terrorist operations, driven by the lethal 
use of small commercial and military drones, as demonstrated in recent conflicts 
(Ukraine, Gaza, the Middle East, etc.). Furthermore, there has been an emergence 
and multiplication of the use of loitering munitions, which are increasingly accessible 
and have even more lethal effects. Likewise, there has been a significant development 
of ballistic, cruise and hypersonic missiles, as well as long-range precision weapons 
launched from the ground or from the air.
Another disturbing phenomenon is the proliferation of incidents at airports, with the 
sighting of commercial drones in their vicinity. The consequent danger of interference 
with navigation systems or even with the aircraft themselves has sometimes forced 
local air traffic to be diverted, for operational safety.
The second outstanding question, and motive for reviewing the former Strategy, is 
the need to constantly assess the threats evolving in previously unknown spheres. The 
emergence of new players in the field of aerospace, some of them beyond the control 
of States and of traditional international structures, is not unheard of, but the rapid 
increase in their number and in their technological and financial capabilities is novel 
and alarming.
The evolution of these and other threats requires concerted effort to ensure 
cybersecurity and the protection of critical entities within the aerospace sector. 
It is essential to maintain the products, applications and services provided by 
space capabilities such as communications satellites, Earth observation satellites, 
meteorological satellites, and positioning, navigation and synchronisation systems.
Thirdly, within the framework of the Strategic Compass objectives, which aim to 
strengthen the European Union as a security provider, in March 2023 the EU Commission 
and the High Representative presented the Joint Communication “European Union 
Space Strategy for Security and Defence” to the European Parliament and the 
Council. Another initiative, the White Paper on European Defence, identified drone/
counter-drone systems and air and missile defence as priority capability areas. The

2025 NASS offers the opportunity to determine national priorities and to influence the 
development of the EU strategy in alignment with these priorities.
The necessary parallelism between European and national policies on aerospace security

update of both strategies. The present review of the Strategy emphasises not only

Finally, the present moment is highly appropriate, considering the evolution of the

undertaken.” With respect to the context of aerospace, it should be taken into account 
that the pre-existing regulatory framework, both national and international, often uses 
the generic term “security”.

The NASS, based on the concepts used by the International Civil Aviation Organisation 
(ICAO), uses the following definitions of safety and security, terms that are related but 
not interchangeable. 
• Safety: A state in which the risks associated with activities related to the 
operation of aircraft, or directly supporting such operation, are reduced and 
controlled to an acceptable level. 
In this definition, the risk to operational safety should be understood as the 
expected probability and severity of the consequences or results of a hazard, 
i.e., the condition or object that could cause or contribute to an aviation incident 
or accident. 
In other words, measures aimed at operational safety seek to proactively 
mitigate operational safety risks before they result in aviation accidents or 
incidents. 
• Security: A condition in which risks and threats that may compromise 
the State’s freedom of action in its sovereign airspace, or where it bears 
responsibility, and  those that may affect the safety of citizens using the air 
domain, are reduced and controlled to an acceptable level. It includes the 
following elements:     
 ȃ Surveillance of outer space, surveillance and control of airspace, and 
intervention when necessary.
 ȃ Defence against hostile or potentially hostile actions within the air domain.
 ȃ Contributing to air safety and defence both domestically and abroad, 
within the framework of the international organisations to which Spain 
belongs.
 ȃ Combating the communication of false information that might compromise 
the operational safety of an aircraft, whether in flight or on the ground, or

the safety of passengers, crew, ground personnel and/or the public at an 
airport or on the premises of a civil aviation facility.  
Although these concepts are distinct, the aviation community recognises that many 
risks concerning safety, security or other areas like health security (such as the 
COVID-19 pandemic) cannot be addressed in isolation due to their interconnectedness 
and cross-cutting impact. Therefore, collaboration across different areas is essential. 
An example of the need to jointly manage operational safety and security is the case 
of higher airspace operations (HAO), which have been provisionally defined as those 
conducted above the altitudes at which aviation services are currently provided (up to 
approximately 55,000 feet). High-altitude pseudo-satellites, remotely piloted vehicles 
operating at high altitudes, balloons, and supersonic or hypersonic aircraft or missiles 
can all operate at these altitudes, crewed or otherwise, autonomously or with varying 
degrees of automation. High-altitude operations pose risks to operational safety (to 
other air traffic, third parties, or persons and property on the ground), but the risks to 
security and defence are no less significant.
Above these altitudes, it is difficult to exercise sovereignty due to the limitations 
of surveillance and control systems and of air defence operations. Detection by 
conventional radar, whether ground or aircraft-based, is difficult at these altitudes, and 
the high speed of aircraft can make interception difficult. However, if left unchecked, 
HAO by external forces pose a real threat.
In view of the particularities of the space domain with respect to the air domain, the 
ICAO-defined terms must be adapted to maintain their functionality and relevance.
In accordance with the definitions proposed by the United Nations Office for Outer 
Space Affairs (UNOOSA), the concept of “safety” could be applied to operational 
safety in space, viewed as the result of measures aimed at preventing accidental or 
unintentional damage that impedes normal operation and at mitigating potential 
damage to space assets. On the other hand, “security” would refer to protection 
against threats to all aspects of space systems, in order to prevent deliberate damage.

jurisdiction in the areas of security and defence, particularly with respect to space

CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS OF THE AIR AND 
SPACE DOMAINS   
GENERAL ASPECTS OF AEROSPACE AND RELATED ACTIVITIES 
The development of commercial aviation, the important economic activity generated 
within airspace and in its use, and the harmful effects on our society that can result 
from the control and utilisation of this environment by antagonists, even in peacetime, 
make the domains of air and space a fundamental area of State action.
The National Security Act of 2015 includes the security of terrestrial airspace and outer 
space among the areas of special interest for national security that require specific 
attention. These spaces have no physical boundaries and are characterised, among 
other parameters, by their accessibility. In the case of outer space, this is facilitated by 
the increasing use of low-altitude orbits.

           
National Aerospace Security Strategy 2025 Presidency the Government
24
Outer space, henceforth simply “space,” is a continuous global environment without 
geographical barriers or national sovereignty, governed by its own physical laws 
and only loosely regulated by international treaties. It hosts a growing number of 
capabilities and services crucial to society, security and defence, which in turn has 
intensified the presence and activity of both State and non-State actors.
The environmental complexity of space, together with the diverse interests of 
and relationships among the parties involved, highlights the need for appropriate 
management and regulation. Although some critical aspects of space use, such as the 
assignment of orbits and operating frequencies, are rules governed, it is imperative to 
develop a more detailed framework that effectively regulates the safe and sustainable 
use of space.
Space, particularly Earth orbit, is a competitive, contested and congested environment, 
requiring specific capabilities to guarantee safe and secure access and thus contribute 
to decision-making autonomy. These circumstances have led Spain, the European 
Union and NATO to recognise its importance as a specific operational domain for 
security and defence.
Security in Aerospace  (terrestrial and extra-terrestrial space) must be addressed 
through coordinated policies, measures and actions to protect and ensure freedom of 
action consistent with the proper use, integrity, functionality and sustainability of both 
domains.
Spain, given its capabilities and economic position, is one of Europe’s main partners in 
the aeronautical and space sectors. Each of these is crucial to the nation’s economic 
development and security, and consequently, it is essential to guarantee access and 
freedom of action in both areas.
Aerospace assets are considered critical entities and essential services as established by 
the European directives on the resilience of critical entities (CER) and on cybersecurity 
(NIS2). The protection of these and associated infrastructures, as well as the proper 
management and safeguarding of the integrity of capabilities, users and services, is 
essential for the functioning and development of society.

The lack of a physical boundary between the air and space domains makes international 
agreement on its definition difficult; hence, existing regulations on the matter are limited 
and imprecise. For the purposes of the Strategy, and to facilitate the coordination 
of operations in both domains, in this paper the theoretical and academic boundary 
between the two domains is considered to be located at an altitude of approximately 
100 km, on the so-called Kármán line.
The rules governing the use of air and space are based on substantially different 
principles. While national airspace is subject to State sovereignty, outer space is 
considered the common heritage of humanity, according to international treaties. 
However, the principle of non-appropriation of space, enshrined in United Nations 
treaties on the matter, is subject to interpretation and could be challenged in the future 
by national laws that facilitate the commercial exploration and use of space resources.
The exploration and exploitation of space represents a source of knowledge, drives 
the development of new technologies, provides applications that have become 
indispensable for  quality of life in society, creates highly qualified jobs, and has 
significant implications for security and defence. The demand for services related to the 
use of space will continue to increase. Accordingly, the State must support the sector, 
seeking the greatest possible strategic autonomy, and reducing its technological and 
industrial dependence on other powers in the development of space capabilities and 
the provision of services essential to the functioning and economy of the country.
The importance of airspace is not limited to the rapid and efficient transport of people 
and goods; this domain is also fundamental to global integration, contributing to 
economic growth and connectivity between nations. Like space, it is a strategic sector 
that generates highly skilled employment and vital investment in technology and 
innovation, and is a fundamental driver of exports.
The growing demand for aeronautical services requires us to reduce their environmental 
impact and improve operational efficiency. In consequence, State support is needed to 
encourage the development of proprietary technologies and to reduce our external 
dependence for critical components.

PATTERNS OF CHANGE IN THE DOMAINS OF AIR AND SPACE    
The international context is undergoing a period of great uncertainty, although certain 
clear trends are emerging that will shape the global order in the medium to long term. 
This combination of changes and constants is shaping a new paradigm in political, 
economic and geopolitical spheres.   
FACTORS OF UNCERTAINTY 
Two factors make it difficult to contemplate the present time in terms of traditional 
patterns of competition among States: 
• Challenges of a global nature  – primarily climate change, but also others 
such as pandemics, certain technological aspects or nuclear proliferation 
– can only be addressed through international cooperation, which is more 
difficult in a context of rivalries. These foster mistrust, which incentivises 
States not to cede relative advantages, even if the price of non-cooperation 
is higher.
• The accelerating adoption of new technologies , the consequences of 
which are unpredictable. The aerospace sector is just one arena within this 
transformation, but it is interconnected with many others. The weakening 
of our industrial and technological base may lead to difficult choices 
between acquiring capabilities (created by foreign actors, public or private) 
or maintaining higher levels of autonomy and sovereignty. 
The digital revolution – with all its corollaries, including the collectivisation of 
knowledge through the Internet, its automatic replication via artificial intelligence 
(AI), the blurring of the virtual and physical realms, quantum computing, mass data 
collection and its behavioural predictive power, the manipulation of information and 
images, and the automation of warfare – impacts all areas of international relations 
and is a factor of uncertainty.

Technology has become a source of power, rivalry and competition, in many spheres 
of life, including the military. It has transformed the fabric of the global economy, its 
infrastructure, and the very concepts of value creation, fostering the globalisation of 
its services (led by the new technology giants) and highlighting the need for regulatory 
harmonisation, a goal that conflicts with growing geopolitical fragmentation.  
NEW CONSTANTS  
There is a growing use of force outside the scope of international law, the influence 
of the Indo-Pacific is on the rise, and Europe is facing increasing conflicts. At the same 
time, multilateralism is weakening and the Global South is gaining economic and 
demographic prominence, albeit with a worrying rise in transactional diplomacy and a 
downplaying of international law and human rights.
In the economic sphere, globalisation has heightened concerns for resilience and 
economic security. Risk mitigation is being prioritised over freedom to trade, creating 
barriers and disputes in global commerce. Businesses can no longer isolate themselves 
from international politics, and are becoming more present in States’ foreign policies.
Security is no longer dissociable from industrial policy. We are witnessing a shift in 
mentality among some of the world’s leading industrial powers, who treat the 
competitiveness of their companies as a strategic requirement as well as an economic 
asset. This new mentality poses serious dilemmas for economic models like those of 
Spain, and Europe as a whole, which are based on open trade, free markets and free 
competition.
In line with these underlying geopolitical trends, there is growing competition in space 
among countries such as China, Russia, India and the United States, which are investing 
heavily in advanced space technologies.
The above are some of the underlying trends that, apart from turning points like the 
war in Ukraine and future unforeseeable events, are decisively transforming the world 
and intertwining geopolitics and economics to an increasing extent.

PATTERNS OF CHANGE 
From an economic perspective , the aeronautical industry is an economic mainstay, 
generating employment and contributing massively to our GDP. The sector’s 
competitiveness must be maintained through investment in innovation and 
improvements throughout the value chain.
To support the sector’s growth and improve safety, airports must be modernised and 
advanced air traffic management systems developed. Moreover, such infrastructure 
investments boost tourism and trade.
Governments in all major developed countries provide financial incentives and support 
to their domestic industries to encourage innovation and protect employment in the

Two fundamental laws, the Air Navigation Act 48/1960 and the Air Safety Act 21/2003, 
continue to shape the legal framework for managing Spanish airspace. Both laws, 
which implement ministerial powers, have been updated numerous times to adapt

In the European context, the basic references in this respect are Regulation (EU)

The fundamental task of Spain’s law enforcement forces is to protect the free 
exercise of rights and freedoms and to guarantee public safety and security. This 
precept is set out both in the Spanish Constitution and in Organic Law 2/1986 on

inescapably requires the adequate protection of airspace. In this respect, the 
responsibilities of law enforcement bodies include public safety at airports, the 
custody and protection of airport infrastructure and the supervision of compliance 
with immigration regulations and with the State’s fiscal safeguards.

a. The surveillance, control and defence of Spanish sovereign airspace, and the

The European Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) has proposed a roadmap for operations

REGULATORY FRAMEWORK FOR SAFETY AND SECURITY IN 
SPACE 
The lack of adequate legislation represents a significant challenge to the governance 
of space and to the protection of national interests. Currently, there is intense 
competition for licences to use space frequencies in orbits, especially low earth orbits 
(LEO), which provide nations with strategic and operational superiority. The growing 
participation of non-governmental entities in the sector (known as New Space) 
highlights the urgency of establishing a national regulatory framework that regulates 
space activities, ensures the necessary legal certainty, and aligns the use of space with 
national priorities, while facilitating legislative development at the international level.
The main space treaties date back to the Cold War, with the 1967 Outer Space Treaty 
being the cornerstone of space governance. Spain has ratified key agreements, such 
as the 1963 Moscow Treaty, the 1968 Agreement on the Rescue of Astronauts, the 1972 
Convention on International Liability, and the 1974 Convention on the Registration 
of Space Objects. A new Royal Decree is currently being processed to update Royal 
Decree 278/1995, which establishes the Registry provided for in the 1974 Convention 
on the Registration of Space Objects in Spain and adapts it to the evolution of the 
sector.
At the international level, the United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA) 
and the Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) coordinate 
peaceful cooperation in the use of space. Furthermore, the Space 2030 Agenda 
promotes strategic partnerships for sustainable space development.
To mitigate the problem of space debris, the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination 
Committee (IADC) recommends measures such as the “25-Year Rule” and sustainable 
satellite design.
Spain has been a signatory to the Artemis Accords since 2023, which establish 
principles for responsible space exploration, including transparency, the peaceful use 
of space and the safe disposal of space vehicles. Adherence to these principles makes 
operations in the space environment safer and more predictable.

THE NATIONAL AEROSPACE SECURITY STRATEGY, 
STRENGTHENING OUR SECURITY  
TWO DOMAINS, A SINGLE SECURITY STRATEGY 
Any object that could reach the Earth’s surface from space and pose a threat would 
inevitably have to transit through the air from the orbital space of origin. Therefore, 
the functions of surveillance, detection, identification and classification of such 
objects must be extended to determine the most appropriate response, as well as 
to collaborate in the attribution of actions that, intentional or otherwise, endanger 
aerospace security.
Advances and falling costs in space technology have led to a proliferation of private 
companies and public-private partnerships with different interests in space. These 
partnerships have exponentially increased the number of annual operations carried 
out in this field, while simultaneously increasing the threats posed to both aviation and 
space operations.
With ever more objects orbiting or transitioning from orbital regimes to controlled 
airspace, a higher degree of coordination will be required to minimise the risk of 
collision, and more manoeuvres to avoid impact will be needed, whenever this is an 
option. In both cases, the consequences are negative, as they can lead to increased 
restrictions on the use of airspace, generate difficulties in coordination mechanisms 
and reduce the useful life of orbiting systems.
In the other area of interest, threats to space-based positioning, navigation, timing 
and communications systems (which are essential to the operational safety of air 
traffic management) make it essential to conduct a comprehensive view of aerospace

INTRODUCTION TO THE CONCEPTS OF RISK AND THREAT IN 
THE DOMAINS OF AIR AND SPACE 
The 2019 National Aerospace Security Strategy defined the concepts of threat and 
challenge, distinguishing the two according to intent. However, in the present review 
of the question, our approach to these definitions avoids any initial attempt to assess 
the intent or otherwise of an event due to the difficulty of unequivocally demonstrating 
this in certain scenarios.
For the purposes of this Strategy, therefore, “Threat” is defined as a situation or event, 
whether natural or of human origin, and whether accidental or deliberate, that, if 
materialised, could cause damage or loss to the assets to be protected.
Accordingly, in the domains of air and space, threats are considered transversal to the 
contexts of safety and security.

           
National Aerospace Security Strategy 2025 Presidency the Government
54
By contrast, “Risk” is defined as the result of combining the expected probability 
and impact on an asset to be protected, should a threat materialise. This approach 
facilitates risk analysis and management, and subsequent decision making, enabling 
an appropriate, prioritised and anticipated response to be made. In risk management, 
the risk level is determined from the combination of probability and resultant impact.
In view of the current proliferation of dual-use technologies, specific mechanisms are 
needed for determining threats, considering not only the presence of the technology, 
but also the behaviour of the parties involved. Services that are essential to public 
safety and well-being and to the security of air operations may depend on space 
assets, which in certain cases adds complexity to categorising threats as exclusive to 
the air or space domain. Moreover, the assets operating in these areas may present 
vulnerabilities.
Although the Strategy addresses the specificities of the air and space domains 
separately, in terms of threats they must really be considered interconnected domains, 
due to their nature and because of their shared vulnerabilities to transversal effects in 
certain cases. In the following, we present a non-exhaustive list of threats that, while 
each is unique and has a different nature and origin, are common to the domains of air 
and space.

ARMED CONFLICTS 
Growing strategic rivalry and economic decoupling are increasing global instability and 
heightening competition for control of resources, which could lead to an escalation of 
the use of force and give rise to armed conflict. The lack of effective peaceful resolution 
mechanisms, together with the rise of unilateralism, generates an environment of 
mistrust, which increases the risk of a war between major powers due to miscalculations 
or misunderstandings.
Armed conflicts, in addition to the obvious threat they pose to the aerospace 
capabilities involved, also endanger other assets that share the same physical operating 
environment and may suffer the collateral effects of operations, even if they take place 
in remote areas. Such actions can result in the closure of airspace or the disruption 
of international routes, requiring the redesign and coordination of flight routes to 
avoid risks. Space, too, has become a vulnerable strategic domain, where satellites are 
exposed to interference or attack, highlighting the need to strengthen international 
cooperation in the protection of these assets.

           
National Aerospace Security Strategy 2025 Presidency the Government
56
The following are some potential threats to aerospace systems in the context of armed 
conflict: 
Kinetic physical attacks against air/space systems (for example, anti-satellite weapons) 
or against the infrastructure from which they operate (bases and airports, command 
and control centres, ground-based control and tracking centres, spaceports). These 
attacks seek the partial or total destruction of the target through direct impact, 
temporarily or permanently affecting its capacity. They may be carried out using not 
only weapons designed for this purpose, but also weapons capable of accurately 
reaching objects in space from the ground.
Non-kinetic physical attacks against air/space systems or the infrastructure that enables 
their operation and control. These attacks, while not seeking direct physical contact 
with the target, aim to cause physical damage, degrading its capabilities. They may be 
carried out using directed energy weapons (lasers, particle beams, radio frequency), 
and used from the ground, in the air or from space. Depending on the characteristics, 
they can cause temporary or permanent damage to the systems targeted.
Electromagnetic attacks  that use energy to secure the use of the electromagnetic 
spectrum for their own benefit and deny it to the adversary. These attacks, as in the 
previous cases, can affect air and space systems and ground-based infrastructure, 
including the radio frequency links that facilitate their capabilities.
This category includes various techniques such as spoofing (capturing, altering and 
retransmitting a signal to confuse the receiver) and interference or jamming (masking 
or obstructing the signal at the receiver). These types of techniques are frequently 
used to disrupt communications and PNT (positioning, navigation and timing) services. 
The degradation or unavailability of these services may impact on all areas of national 
security.
While the nature of these attacks does not directly seek the physical destruction of the 
target, this outcome often occurs, potentially causing loss of human life and the loss 
of the asset itself. Moreover, after the asset is captured it may be subjected to reverse 
engineering.
57
National Aerospace Security Strategy 2025Presidency the Government
Nuclear attacks ; such attacks generate an electromagnetic pulse that affects all 
electrical components, and have great physical destructive power with devastating 
short-, medium-, and long-term consequences for the affected area and its inhabitants. 
If one of these weapons were detonated in space, the impacts would be indiscriminate 
on multiple satellites, potentially generating a chain reaction (the Kessler effect) 
with unforeseen consequences for the interests of the launching State, and possibly 
catastrophic repercussions for the entire Earth.
Weapons of mass destruction can use aerial means to transport an attack or to serve 
as a vector for this purpose. In this respect, drones, at relatively low cost, can reach 
targets that were previously limited to missiles.
While the materialisation of one or more of these threats is a real possibility in the 
context of armed conflicts, they may also occur in other scenarios, given the existence 
of non-State actors with solid sources of financing and access to capabilities (including 
nuclear or other agents of mass destruction) that can be used against certain aerospace 
assets and the facilities that support their operation.
In response to these dangers, the Spanish Air and Space Force provides ongoing 
protection for national airspace through its air defence systems, based on continuous 
surveillance (24/7) through a network of radars, command and control centres, and 
armed combat aircraft on permanent alert, ready to identify, intercept and neutralise 
any aerial threat that may arise. This air defence system forms part of NATO’s Integrated 
Air and Missile Defence System, via the Combined Air Operations Centre, located at 
Torrejón de Ardoz Air Base (Madrid), which controls this mission for all of southern 
Europe, from the Canary Islands to Türkiye.

NATURAL CATASTROPHES    
Natural disasters pose a significant threat to the aerospace sector due to their ability to 
disrupt operations, damage critical facilities and operating personnel, and jeopardise 
the safety of aircraft and space systems.
Infrastructure located on the Earth’s surface is vulnerable to various natural 
phenomena, both geophysical and meteorological in origin. The former include 
earthquakes and tsunamis, while the latter include floods, intense rainfall caused by 
high-level depressions (known as DANAs in Spain), hailstorms or very strong winds, 
and tornadoes. The damage caused by these phenomena can paralyse aerospace 
operations for days or weeks, causing significant economic losses and disruptions to 
the global supply chain (fuel, electricity, materials, etc.).
Other types of natural phenomena, such as volcanic ash, thunderstorms, frost and fog, 
can directly affect the safety of aerospace operations.

in orbit or re-enter the atmosphere with the consequent danger to the safety of air

SUPPLY CHAIN INSECURITY   
Some nations have developed strategies aimed at blocking or restricting third-party 
access to certain technologies or raw materials, often essential for supply chain 
procurement. This has led to abuse of their monopolistic position over specific services 
and products (e.g., rare earths used in high-tech equipment), or to their preventing the

This scenario penalises the competitiveness of the national and European aerospace

Boosting domestic industry and imposing export controls might be key policies to 
successfully negotiate and overcome permit denials. Furthermore, classifying certain 
aerospace technologies as critical to national security would strengthen negotiations 
in the face of supply blockades, improving production and reducing dependence on 
other countries.

supply of critical components and vital aeronautical capabilities. Accordingly, Spain 
should produce a catalogue of technologies of strategic importance for its internal 
development, and launch innovation programmes to achieve sovereignty and 
autonomy in these areas, in conjunction with associated international programmes at 
the European level.

TERRORISM, VIOLENT EXTREMISM, ORGANISED CRIME AND 
MAJOR FELONIES  
The threat of terrorist attacks against the commercial aviation sector, military 
aerospace capabilities and/or their infrastructure, while always present and never out 
of consideration, is especially acute in the current context, in which there has been a 
significant increase in the overall terrorist threat, mainly arising from the conflict in the 
Middle East. Global terrorist organisations are now particularly active, attempting to 
plan and execute high-impact actions.
Among such dangers, plots against the airline industry are once again at the forefront 
of terrorists’ ambitions. The September 11 attacks in the United States in 2001 clearly 
demonstrated that attacking the airline industry is one of the most profitable targets 
for terrorist groups, both in terms of operational success, in the number of casualties 
produced, and, above all, due to its capacity to generate terror and the need for 
multimillion-dollar investments to improve the sector’s security.
Jihadist groups have had, still have and will continue to have a primary interest in 
planning and executing attacks against the commercial aviation sector, and their 
techniques, tactics and procedures have been modified over time and adapted to the 
circumstances, capabilities and vulnerabilities detected at each time and place.
Airspace can be exploited by terrorist groups to carry out attacks, whether through 
the hijacking of aircraft or through the use of aircraft for terrorist acts. However, the 
Spanish Security Forces are equipped to identify potential terrorist threats involving the 
use of airspace and neutralise them before they occur. Collaboration with intelligence 
agencies and international cooperation are key factors in preventing such attacks. The 
development of specific intelligence on air threats, the effective use of early warning 
systems and the performance of joint exercises by the National Police, the Civil Guard 
and the Armed Forces are necessary to ensure a rapid and coordinated response.
Some violent extremist groups seek to disrupt good order and security in airport 
environments by breaching perimeter fences and entering restricted security areas, 
possibly gaining access to the apron where aircraft are parked, in order to carry out 
protests. For the purposes of this Strategy, these types of actions could be considered

and, consequently, on civil aviation.
The use of airspace for illegal activities, such as drug trafficking or smuggling using 
light aircraft or drones, is a constant risk that has been observed and confirmed in 
the activities of organised crime.
The use of drones has expanded dramatically in recent years, in number and in 
diversity. The mere presence of drones in the vicinity of aerospace, civil and military 
infrastructure poses a threat to operations, regardless of the user’s purpose. 
Currently, terrorist groups use drones for reconnaissance and propaganda purposes, 
as transports for explosive devices to be released in the direction of the target, and 
as attack vectors (suicide drones). To date, the drones used by terrorists have mostly

only a matter of time.
Commercial drones have been used to smuggle items, primarily drugs and cell

smuggling weapons into prisons using these types of drones, equipped with GPS and

severely compromise prison security. The use of aerospace-based means for escape 
also poses a significant threat.

crash it into a target. Finally, Pegaso teams are deployed throughout the country.

CYBER THREATS 
The infrastructure of a complex airport environment, including aspects related 
to terrestrial functions and the centres that manage and regulate air traffic, are a 
high-value, high-impact target for those who, through cyberattacks, seek to cause 
chaos or exploit the information stored on their servers. Hence, the work of the 
agencies responsible for preventing these attacks, as well as quickly restoring damage 
should it occur, is vitally important.
So far, relatively few attacks have directly targeted civil aviation, but in recent years, 
terrorists have shown increased interest in cyberattacks and a general intent to carry 
them out. At present, hacking activities by extremists are characterised by relatively 
basic denial-of-service attacks. No examples of specific terrorist cyberthreats against 
aircraft, airports or airbases have been detected, nor are there any indications that 
terrorist groups have acquired or are developing advanced capabilities or that they 
currently consider this an effective way to carry out a mass-effect attack, especially 
when compared to more obvious and proven attack methodologies. Nevertheless, the 
possibility of such an attack in the future cannot be ruled out.
Air defence command, control and communications systems must address a similar 
threat, with the added complexity that it may originate from State or non-State actors.
In the space domain, cyberattacks represent a growing threat, capable of seriously 
affecting both terrestrial infrastructure and orbiting space systems.
Given the hyperconnectivity of our systems and our dependence on networks and 
information systems, if appropriate protective measures are not implemented the 
vulnerability to cyberattacks may be high. These could have devastating consequences, 
compromising the safety, operability and reliability of critical space services and even 
affect national security.
A cyberattack targeting space assets could disrupt satellite operations, preventing 
them from fulfilling their function or impeding their control and tracking. This, in turn, 
could provoke a trajectory alteration, leading to mission failure and the loss of the 
asset. Moreover, an altered trajectory might pose a risk to other satellites, requiring

operations to avoid collisions; should these occur, the State responsible for the hijacked 
object might be legally liable for the consequences.
Cyberattacks could also be conducted to steal, leak or manipulate sensitive data 
from specific targets, endangering national security and compromising strategic 
information. The alteration of critical data can lead to poor decisions, impacting the 
safety and efficiency of space operations. The ready access to constantly updated 
malware vehicles and the integration of generative AI into attacks also broaden the 
threat spectrum, making it accessible to less skilled actors. 
ESPIONAGE 
Aerospace espionage is one of the most insidious and difficult-to-quantify, and 
sometimes attribute, threats, and its effects can be profound and far-reaching. It is 
not limited to military or government contexts, as a means of obtaining classified 
or sensitive information, but can extend into business, scientific and research fields 
through new techniques, tactics and procedures, which in many cases are linked to 
information technologies.
Espionage can enable the perpetrator to neutralise technological and operational 
advantages in the national aerospace sector and thus provoke relative disadvantages, 
especially with respect to disruptive, decisive technologies in fields essential to security 
and defence.
Multispectral observation satellites can perform the continuous monitoring of ground 
activities and collect critical data on movements and positions, which is essential for 
strategic decision-making. In recent years, small satellites in low-Earth orbits have 
become a valuable resource for the characterisation, monitoring, geolocation and 
tracking of critical entities.
In response to the current proliferation of satellites and the need to determine their 
threat potential, specific systems are being developed to observe other objects 
from space, providing non-earth imaging. In addition, existing capabilities of Earth

Communications interception is another common form of espionage. To combat this

encryption elements and, to the extent possible, sensitive information should not be 
transmitted through infrastructure lacking such protection.
Regarding industrial espionage, measures must be adopted to prevent unauthorised

In this regard, one of the most common vulnerabilities the sector faces arises from 
the high level of cooperative development with third countries that may be more 
industrially advanced and a lack of protection against technological absorption 
measures developed by these countries. To combat this danger, we must determine 
which technologies are shareable and which should be considered essential for national 
security and protected accordingly.

THREATS FROM INCREASED SPACE ACTIVITY 
With the introduction of new uses of space, there has been a corresponding increase in 
the number of actors involved and in levels of space activity, a pattern of change that is 
expected to continue in the coming decades. The exploration of new ways to monetise 
space operations is resulting in an entire ecosystem of opportunities together with 
traditional space-use activities. These new possibilities include space tourism, mining, 
colonisation and more.
Additionally, within the traditional uses of space, activity is also increasing with the 
deployment of mega-constellations of mini-satellites, and the resulting increase in the 
number and types of launches, some of which enable components to return to Earth’s 
surface for reuse.
This increase in space activity poses a threat to the long-term sustainability of space 
operations if the existing regulatory framework is not adapted to address changing 
circumstances.
For example, the orbits closest to Earth are now saturated with operational space 
objects, and also with space debris.
This space ‘junk’, regardless of its nature, significantly endangers current and future 
space operations, with repercussions on exploration, research and many other 
activities. The re-entry of space objects can also affect the safety of operations in 
airspace and on Earth’s surface.     
The contamination of space with this debris and the overcrowding of space assets in 
the most profitable orbits limits compromises the notion of free and safe access to 
space for all. Furthermore, impacts of space debris on operational assets are occurring, 
heightening the risk of the Kessler effect (a cascading space debris scenario where 
the density of objects in low Earth orbit becomes so high that collisions become 
increasingly likely, leading to a chain reaction of further collisions and debris creation).
The number of objects in orbit already poses a challenge for Earth-based space 
observation missions, as their reflections and obstacles prevent dark sky observations.

that this initiative has instrumental value for the development of science and is closely 
linked to the long- and medium-term sustainability of space activities.
Contributing to operational sustainability is a global responsibility, and the measures 
to achieve this goal offer new opportunities, but also new threats, stemming from the 
dual nature of many space capabilities.
Advances in in-orbit servicing are revolutionising space asset management, combining 
technological opportunities with strategic challenges to safety. These operations, 
designed to extend satellite lifespans or enhance their capabilities, involve technologies 
such as robotic arms, capture systems and rendezvous and proximity operations 
(RPOs). However, their dual nature poses geopolitical risks, as the same techniques 
could be used to alter the trajectories of third-party satellites.
Space intelligence activities have also been developed to observe space assets from 
space, using space surveillance and tracking systems. These make it possible to track 
and identify space vehicles for extended periods of time, using orbiting space assets, 
probably with the intention of carrying out intelligence operations, such as signal 
capture.
Rising levels of space activity also require enhanced coordination with airspace 
activity, as additional risks to operations may be can generated if adequate resources 
are not available to accurately track and monitor space assets as they pass through the 
atmosphere during exit and re-entry.

auxiliary industry to provide the necessary components for advances in space, in all 
areas of activity, especially those most vulnerable to intentional attacks. Declarations

and risk analyses.

in the aerospace sector.

ACTION 4: PROMOTE NATIONAL AUTONOMY IN ACCESS TO SPACE

launch space assets (rockets and satellites).

therefore security, over missions and objectives of interest.

territory will also allow us to attract investment and promote experimentation and 
the development of new technologies and initiatives related to space activity, thus 
creating jobs in the sector and helping transform the economy.

ACTION 16: PROMOTE THE DEVELOPMENT OF INTERNATIONAL 
PROTOCOLS FOR THE USE AND TRANSMISSION OF SPACE ALERTS    
Action will be taken to enhance the capabilities of the Spanish Space Surveillance and 
Tracking System, in coordination with activities carried out within the framework of 
the European Space Agency, the European Union, and other areas of Spanish public 
administrations, to include improvements and new functionalities with respect to 
risk analysis and the evaluation of security measures. Specifically, we will address the 
following areas: 
 ȃ Processing capabilities, including cataloguing and capabilities specific to 
space security needs;
 ȃ The capabilities to provide space surveillance and tracking services enabling 
more accurate calculations of object re-entry trajectories, and to detect 
and, where appropriate, attribute potentially dangerous manoeuvres;
 ȃ The coordination and exchange of information with other space security 
centres or services (in areas such as secure communications, secure 
navigation and remote sensing for security);
 ȃ The development and integration of national and international protocols 
for alerts and response to adverse space weather events, and their 
alignment with existing studies and plans for space weather protection.

and the Civil Service; Spanish Space Agency.
