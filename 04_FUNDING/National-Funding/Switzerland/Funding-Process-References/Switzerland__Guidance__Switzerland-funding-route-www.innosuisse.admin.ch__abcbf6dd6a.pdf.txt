1 The funding instrument “Flagship Initiative”  
The purpose of the flagship initiative is to stimulate innovation in areas relevant to a large 
part of the economy or society and to promote transdisciplinary project collaboration. The Ini-
tiative strives for solutions to current or future challenges, which concern several actors 
and/or can only be solved through collaborative work. Innosuisse is therefore defining spe-
cific but broad flagship topics in order to encourage applicants to address issues that are of 
particular interest for the future well-being of the country, and where new solutions and busi-
ness models are required. This new funding instrument is complementary to the purely bot-
tom-up oriented and well-established regular innovation projects. 
Systemic innovation, where value can only be derived if it is synergistically integrated with 
other complementary innovations, and transdisciplinarity, where actors from different disci-
plines collaborate, are key components of this new funding instrument. 
The overall objective is to strengthen the competitiveness of the Swiss economy and/or con-
tribute to the well-being of society by creating new value along the value chain by increasing 
efficiencies, reducing costs or re-solving social relevant challenges in the public interest. 
Under the Flagship Initiative, Innosuisse invites consortia to submit proposals for implement-
ing flagship (Figure 1). Each flagship proposal consists of closely connected and interde-
pendent subprojects (Figure 2). As a rule of thumb, a flagship should consist of three to eight 
sub-projects. Flagship proposals must be submitted by a consortium comprising multiple re-
search and implementation partners from diverse disciplines (Figure 3).

Flagships are characterized by a systemic, transdisciplinary perspective and a focus on prac-
tical solutions. Priority is given to systemic innovation rather than the incremental improve-
ment of existing systems (e.g., merely increasing efficiency or material properties). Instead, 
Flagships aim for long-term transformation, such as integrated mobility management or the 
circular economy. 
Crucially, it is not the result of a single subproject that drives change, but the synergy of inter-
dependent subprojects working together. Therefore, strong transdisciplinary collaboration 
between academia and industry is essential. A proposed Flagship will only be considered if it 
demonstrates that the overall systemic result is greater than the sum of its individual 
parts.

Definition of systemic innovation 
Systemic innovation is a type of innovation where value can only be derived if it is syner-
gistically integrated with other complementary innovations, going beyond the boundaries of 
a single organization. Systemic innovation requires multiple innovations to be coordinated. 
Consequently, the different contributory innovations and organizations are organized to-
gether into a whole system, where the overall success of the emergent innovation cannot 
be attributed to just one sub-innovation or participating organization.1 
 
Definition of transdisciplinarity 
Transdisciplinary is a way of thinking that rises above and beyond the methods of individ-
ual disciplines, seeing multiple levels of meaning rather than simple dichotomies. Transdis-
ciplinarity recognizes the existence of a complex and dynamic reality that transcends the 
boundaries of disciplines and requires a holistic and integrative approach.2

2 Call Announcement 
2.1 Flagship topic of the call 2026 
In an increasingly volatile security and geopolitical environment, physical and digital infra-
structures, mobility systems and security-relevant applications are exposed to ever more 
complex threats. Technological dependencies on vulnerable or externally controlled systems 
can significantly restrict the ability of government, the economy and society to act. Resilient 
and secure key technologies that operate reliably under disrupted or hostile conditions are 
therefore essential to safeguard the long-term functioning of Swiss critical infrastructures and 
national security. 
 
The 2026 Flagship call is established as a joint and co-funded initiative between Innosuisse 
and armasuisse to address these challenges through the development of dual-use technolo-
gies. This partnership aligns the strategic mandates of both organisations to strengthen Swit-
zerland’s resilience and technological sovereignty. Simultaneously, the call is aligned with 
the 2025 armaments policy strategy of the Federal Council.  
 
The Flagship topic of the 2026 call is therefore: 
 
Resilient and Secure Technologies for Defence and Mobility 
 
To address this overarching objective effectively, the 2026 call concentrates on three com-
plementary focus areas. They cover key technological enablers that are of strategic im-
portance both for defence capabilities and for secure, highly available mobility. The Flagships 
are expected to develop systemic solutions that integrate one or several of these areas and 
thus make a measurable contribution to Switzerland’s technological sovereignty and resili-
ence. 
 
The three focus areas of the call are: 
 
1. Resilient positioning, navigation and timing (PNT) 
2. Photonics for communication and sensor applications 
3. Innovative technologies to counteract drones (C-UAV) 
 
The following sections describe these focus areas in more detail.

2.1.1 Resilient positioning, navigation and timing (PNT) 
Precise and reliable positioning, navigation and timing (PNT) systems already form the back-
bone of modern society, the economy, national security and defence. Global navigation sat-
ellite systems (GNSS) such as GPS and Galileo are essential for numerous critical infrastruc-
tures. They not only determine precise location and movement for applications such as 
aviation and vehicle tracking but also provide highly accurate timing signals that synchronize 
critical infrastructures, including telecommunications networks, power grids, and financial 
transaction systems. However, GNSS signals are extremely weak and therefore particularly 
susceptible to radio frequency interference (jamming) and signal manipulation (spoofing). In 
recent years, the frequency of such interference, especially spoofing attacks, has increased 
dramatically. It is estimated that over one thousand flights are unintentionally affected by 
GNSS-spoofing every day. These disruptions are primarily caused by military electronic war-
fare systems operated by state actors in conflict zones, used, for example, to defend against 
drones or to influence GPS-guided munitions.  
 
A GNSS failure is expected to have far-reaching and cascading consequences that go far 
beyond security-critical systems. Given the current vulnerability of GNSS-based PNT solu-
tions, the use of multiple independent, complementary technologies to create a resilient over-
all system is becoming increasingly important. Ensuring future-proof PNT capabilities re-
quires both efforts to enhance the resilience and robustness of existing systems, and the 
development of alternative GNSS-independent technologies that do not rely on exposed in-
frastructures. Such technologies offer significant potential in both civil domains, such as avia-
tion, critical infrastructure, autonomous systems and emergency response, and in the field of 
defence, underlining their dual-use character and their overall economic and societal im-
portance.  
 
Strengthening national PNT resilience across critical civil and security-related applications 
requires a trans- and interdisciplinary approach. The aim of this focus area is to foster sys-
temic innovation that develops and demonstrates sovereign, complementary PNT solutions 
capable of improving GNSS resilience or operating independently of GNSS. This includes 
exploring and integrating a broad range of technologies, such as inertial sensors, quantum-
based timing and navigation methods, precision clocks, innovative antennas, new anti-jam-
ming algorithms and software-based solutions, while remaining open to additional technolo-
gies and novel approaches. Also the integration of cyber resilience and anomaly detection is 
within scope to enhance the robustness and trustworthiness of PNT solutions.

2.1.2 Photonics for communication and sensor applications 
Photonics, the science of manipulating light, is becoming essential for modern defence tech-
nologies. With evolving security threats, defence systems require advanced capabilities for 
rapid threat detection, secure communication, and reliable operation in complex environ-
ments. Switzerland’s strong photonics research and innovative industry make it well-posi-
tioned to develop cutting-edge photonics solutions that will strengthen national defence and 
technological sovereignty. 
 
The aim of the focus area is to develop next-generation photonic components and integrated 
systems specifically tailored for defence applications, while also enabling dual-use innova-
tions with broad industrial impact. In scope are photonics technologies for sensor and com-
munication applications (not limited to the examples below) such as: 
 
• Secure high-bandwidth optical communication technologies, incorporating quan-
tum-safe encryption and anti-jamming features to protect sensitive defence infor-
mation. 
• Quantum key distribution technologies and Quantum photonics techniques, to 
future-proof defence communication systems against emerging security threats. Ena-
bling ultra-secure communication by using quantum mechanics principles to distribute 
encryption keys in a way that any interception attempt can be detected, making them 
highly relevant for protecting sensitive data. 
• Low probability of intercept optical communication channels to minimize detect-
ability and resist electronic warfare and cyber-attacks. 
• High-power, tunable laser sources optimised for LIDAR applications, providing 
precise 3D mapping and target detection even in adverse weather or battlefield condi-
tions. 
• Hyperspectral and thermal imaging sensors to enable detection of concealed 
threats through advanced spectral analysis and to enhance night vision capabilities. 
• Active imaging to image through camouflage, vegetation, smoke and fog. 
• Low-photon techniques and detection of optics for the affordable adoption of 
high-performance photon counting technologies in active and passive imaging includ-
ing advanced SPAD (Single-Photon Avalanche Diode) technologies. Retroreflection 
detection is a powerful means of locating adversary sighting systems. Low-photon 
techniques reduce the probability that retroreflection systems themselves are de-
tected. 
• Low-power neuromorphic imaging and computing optimized for military appli-
cations, where digital readout technology of neuromorphic sensors promises to re-
duce electrooptical/infrared (EO/IR) system costs and power consumption while deliv-
ering greater performance compared to conventional sensor designs. Neuromorphic 
inspired pre-processing framework combining optical-domain and electronic compu-
ting to accelerate onboard image processing. 
 
The call encourages interdisciplinary approaches that leverage Switzerland’s strengths in 
photonics, quantum technologies, space technologies, artificial intelligence, and systems in-
tegration to deliver innovative photonics solutions with relevance for both defence and civilian 
applications.

2.1.3 Innovative technologies to counteract drones (C-UAV) 
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) pose a major challenge to the security of society, the 
economy and critical infrastructure. The rapidly increasing availability and advancing technol-
ogy of drones are creating new opportunities for both civilian and government actors, but at 
the same time they pose substantial risks through hostile use. Counter-UAV (C-UAV) sys-
tems are therefore crucial to effectively mitigate threats related to hybrid conflicts and war-
fare, including espionage, sabotage, and attacks on critical infrastructure. The strategic im-
portance of C-UAV technologies will continue to grow in the coming years and is vital not 
only on the battlefield but also for protecting airports, urban areas and other critical infrastruc-
tures. 
 
Current C-UAV systems face various challenges. Limited detection accuracy for discriminat-
ing drones from other flying objects such as birds and the limited detection range make it dif-
ficult to identify drones. Additionally, the speed and manoeuvrability of drones make it difficult 
to detect drones quickly and the limited response time makes it even more challenging to 
take effective countermeasures in time. The ongoing improvements in drone technology are 
resulting in an evolving threat landscape. Some drones contain signature-reducing technolo-
gies or are equipped with jamming/spoofing protection technologies, making current mitiga-
tion strategies less effective. Furthermore, fibre-optic cable drones, capable of operating over 
distances up to 50 km, have been used in Ukraine, and coordinated drone swarms are ex-
pected to be the next major threat. In addition to the technical and tactical challenges, there 
is also the challenge posed by the asymmetry of costs, which currently favours the attackers. 
The measures required to defend against attacks can far exceed the cost of the attacking 
drone itself. This leads to the consumption of expensive ammunition that is actually designed 
for combating high-value assets. In addition, collateral damage risks arising from the counter-
measures must be reduced. 
 
C-UAV systems consist of three key elements: 
 
• Detection, identification and tracking, through technologies such as advanced 
sensor and radar technologies, radio frequency spectrum monitoring, electro-optical 
and infrared imaging and acoustic detection arrays. 
• Resilient command & control architecture, including multi-modal sensor fusion and 
integration platforms and software-based solutions. 
• Active counter-drone measures, such as directed energy weapons, electronic war-
fare systems, kinetic interceptor systems and drone interceptors. 
 
The development of effective and deployable C-UAV systems requires a transdisciplinary ap-
proach. Only by doing so can solutions be created that meet the high requirements for safety 
and efficiency and consider the legal and ethical challenges. The Flagship Initiative aims to 
promote systemic innovation that contributes significantly to strengthening national resilience 
and protection of the population and infrastructure from hostile drone threats.

2.2 Eligible structure and call specifications 
Partners 
The invitation to tender is addressed to consortia comprising both research3 and implementa-
tion4 partners. The consortium consists of at least three research partners, of which at least 
one is a university of applied sciences, and at least two implementation partners.  
Only research institutions that are recognised as eligible research partners by Innosuisse 
may participate as research partners in a Flagship.  
In order to be eligible as a non-commercial research centre outside of the higher education 
sector and to submit an application for contributions to innovation projects, the research cen-
tre must meet the requirements of Article 5 RIPA and Article 10 of the Innosuisse’s imple-
menting provisions for innovation projects. These requirements are outlined in the following 
information sheet: Information on the requirements and criteria for non-commercial research 
centres outside the higher education sector  
Important: All documents required for non-commercial research centres outside the higher 
education sector must already be submitted at the pre-proposal stage. The final eligibility de-
cision for the same organisation is taken during the proposal phase. If the result of the eligi-
bility review is negative, the funding application for a project is not materially assessed and a 
non-consideration decision is taken. 
The Flagship should only include implementation partners who make a substantial contribu-
tion to the project’s execution and implementation. In addition, for the purposes of this call, 
the Federal Office for Defence Procurement (armasuisse) will not be available as an imple-
mentation partner. 
While Innosuisse does not prescribe the internal organization of the consortium, a Higher Ed-
ucation Institution (HEI) or a non-commercial research institution outside the university sec-
tor5 must act as the Host Institution taking over the role of the funding management body6. 
The Host Institution represents the project partners vis-à-vis Innosuisse during the applica-
tion process and throughout the Flagship's duration. Furthermore, it must appoint a repre-
sentative to serve as the primary point of contact for Innosuisse. 
During the implementation phase, the consortium composition may change (new partners 
joining or existing ones withdrawing), provided that the original objectives remain unaffected 
and contractual conditions are upheld. Any such changes require prior written approval from 
Innosuisse and, where necessary, a corresponding contract amendment.

Dual-Use requirement  
Flagship proposals submitted in the framework of the Call 2026 and their targeted innova-
tions must be designed to provide a clear dual-use perspective, i.e. they must be relevant for 
both civil and defence-related applications. Projects that pursue a purely defence or a purely 
civil single-use objective are not eligible for funding under this call. This dual-use character 
will be explicitly queried in the application forms and must be substantiated by the consortium 
in the (pre-)proposal. 
 
Ethics and research security 
Ethics and knowledge-/research security play an important role in this call.  
Innosuisse promotes innovations that meet ethical standards, which translates in behaviour 
and intentions not harming the dignity of humans and animals, life, health, freedom, property, 
the environment, or peaceful coexistence. 
Knowledge & research security refers to the prevention of the unwanted transfer of sensitive 
information, know-how, and technology, the mitigation of foreign interference in higher edu-
cation and research, and the reduction of dependencies that could endanger national secu-
rity and competitiveness. The aim of knowledge & research security is to protect core scien-
tific values, innovative ideas, ensure that international cooperation remains ethical and safe, 
and safeguard national interests and values. 
Project partners must duly consider these aspects in the design and conduct of their project 
as well as in their risk analysis (ethics and knowledge & research security by design). Spe-
cific questions on ethics and research security will be integrated into the application forms 
and the evaluation process. Innosuisse may carry out background checks if needed. 
 
Duration  
There are no strict conditions regarding the duration of a Flagship. However, it is anticipated 
that a Flagship will exceed the duration of a standard Innosuisse innovation project due to its 
systemic approach and transdisciplinary nature. 
 
Budget 
The regulations governing Innosuisse innovation projects apply (see Chapter 3).

3 Finances 
3.1 General principles 
Innosuisse funding covers the personnel costs of research partners, including overhead and 
employer contributions, as well as material costs. Additionally, a dedicated portion of the 
budget may be allocated to management and coordination of the overall flagship initiative. 
 
3.2 Eligible costs 
Personnel costs 
Personnel costs are eligible according to the Innosuisse funding ordinance (Art. 9, 10 and 
12) and the implementing provisions for innovation projects (Art. 15 to 17). Innosuisse fund-
ing covers the effective wage costs of the research partner incl. overhead and employer con-
tribution.  
The personnel costs are calculated either using evidence of the gross salaries paid or the ap-
plicable notional hourly rates according to article 16 and 17 of the implementing provisions 
for innovation projects. 
Material costs 
Material costs are eligible according to the Innosuisse funding ordinance (Art. 9) and the im-
plementing provisions for funding innovation projects (Art. 18). Only costs that are actually 
incurred and are absolutely necessary for the proper execution of the flagship are taken into 
account. All the planned material costs need to be included in the proposal.  
Coordination costs 
Certain coordination costs can be covered within this category which necessarily arise due to 
a coordination effort between the project partners, which is particularly high compared to or-
dinary national innovation projects, and which are not covered by personnel or material costs 
according to the implementing provisions for funding innovation projects (Art. 19).

3.3 Financing conditions 
Innosuisse covers a certain amount of the direct costs of a flagship. The funds provided by 
Innosuisse flow exclusively and directly to the research partners and never to the implemen-
tation partners. The legal basis allows an Innosuisse contribution of 40 to 60%. The imple-
mentation partners contribute to the total costs of the flagship to the extent of 40 to 60% of 
the eligible direct overall project costs. This contribution comprises own work and services as 
well as a financial contribution to the research partners. The financial contribution amount to 
at least 5% of the overall project costs according to the Innosuisse funding ordinance (Art. 
11).

by Innosuisse. armasuisse is not involved in the evaluation of applications or in the funding

4.4 Evaluation criteria7 
• Innovation degree: the innovative content of the project, measured against the current 
state of the science and the available solutions for the needs being addressed 
Is the scientific and economic/societal approach new? Is it not only «incremental» but 
also «systemic» innovation, which means, does the scientific and economic/societal ap-
proach contribute to change a system in the long term? 
• Value creation and sustainability in Switzerland: the potential for effective implemen-
tation of the project results and the anticipated value creation for the Swiss economy or 
society; the contribution to the sustainable development of society, the economy and the 
environment; strengthening security and defence through resilient, trustworthy and fu-
ture-oriented solutions. 
Does the implementation of the research results on the market have a positive influence 
on the competitive situation of the implementation partner(s)? 
Does successful implementation demonstrably reduce (social) costs and/or create eco-
nomic benefit (e.g. increasing security, increasing the quality of life, reducing injustice, 
increasing biodiversity), which leads to a positive impact on the future well-being of the 
country? 
• Flagship set-up 
• the quality of the project planning, qualitative and quantitative goals and imple-
mentation plans for generating the intended economic or social benefit  
• the abilities of the personnel; Do the project partners have the necessary compe-
tences for the execution and the intended implementation on the market? Is the 
consortium well positioned across disciplines (transdisciplinarity)? 
• the cost-benefit ratio.
